*The sixth in a series of essays on the veiling of women, following St. Paul's argument in 1 Corinthians 11. This essay treats verses 13 through 15.*

> Yourselves judge: doth it become a woman not covered to pray unto God? Neither doth nature itself teach you, that a man indeed if he nourish his hair, it is an ignominy for him? But if a woman nourish her hair, it is a glory for her, because hair is given her for a veil?
> (1 Cor 11:13-15, Rheims 1582)

With the main edifice now completed, from the foundation of tradition to the rafters of the angels, St. Paul turns towards stocking the armory and furnishing the building. The reasons he has given are complete in themselves, and what follows is not so much a new proof as a laying in of supplies, so that the household may be defended and may dwell comfortably in what has been built.

The tone of St. Paul has changed here. As we saw in the first essay, St. Paul had already delivered this practice to the Corinthians by word of mouth, and they ought to have kept it without needing the reasons spelled out. Now, having been made to write a whole treatise on a matter they should have known before, he turns the question back upon them, and one can almost hear the rebuke in it. St. Chrysostom hears it too:

> Judge in yourselves: is it comely that a woman pray unto God uncovered? Again he places them as judges of the things said, which also he did respecting the idol-sacrifices. For as there he saith, judge ye what I say: so here, judge in yourselves: and he hints something more awful here. For he says, that the affront here passes on unto God: although thus indeed he doth not express himself, but in something of a milder and more enigmatical form of speech: is it comely that a woman pray unto God uncovered?
> (Chrysostom, Homilies on First Corinthians 26.5, trans. Library of the Fathers, 1839)

St. Thomas says that St. Paul here submits the matter to his hearers "after the manner of one who is confident that he has sufficiently proved his point" (Aquinas, Commentary on 1 Corinthians 11, lect. 3, n. 619, trans. Larcher). He has built a strong and principled case, and now, as it were, he is preparing to unleash the fury. The affront passes on unto God, and we need not draw this out at great length, for it is plain enough: an offense against nature is an offense against God, since, as St. Chrysostom said in a passage we read in the third essay, "when I say Nature, I mean God. For He it is Who created Nature" (Chrysostom, Homilies on First Corinthians 26.4).

## The Law Written in Nature

What, then, does St. Paul mean by 'nature'? St. Thomas answers:

> Thirdly, he shows whence they should derive their judgment, namely, from nature itself; and this is what he says: Does not nature itself teach you? By 'nature' he means the 'natural inclination' in women to take care of their hair, which is a natural covering, but not in men. This inclination is shown to be natural, because it is found in the majority. But it is taught by nature, because it is a work of God; just as in a picture one is instructed about the skill of the artist. Therefore, Isaiah (24:5) says against certain people: 'They have transgressed the law, violated the statutes, broken the everlasting covenant,' i.e., the natural law.
> (Aquinas, Commentary on 1 Corinthians 11, lect. 3, n. 619, trans. Larcher)

Nature here is the law of nature, the law which God has written into the things he has made, so that one may learn from the work something of the mind of the Workman. The hair of the woman is a natural sign, but like every natural sign it hints toward the supernatural, for the order it points to is the order of Eden, and Eden itself lays the foundation of a law which is according to liberty. St. James speaks of the one "that hath looked in the law of perfect liberty, and hath remained in it" (Jas 1:25, Rheims 1582), and the law of nature, rightly understood, is a law of that kind, for it does not bind us against our nature, but according to it.

Yet we must ask which nature teaches, whether nature as it was in Eden or nature as it is after the Fall. The answer is both, as we have spoken of before in climbing back from the Fall towards Eden. Nature in its origin so teaches, for the order of the man and the woman was set down before sin ever entered the world. But even after the Fall, wounded as it is, nature still teaches at a basic level, for the inclination of the woman to keep and care for her long hair has not been lost, and in it the lesson remains, however much the page itself has been smeared.

Tertullian gives a useful witness, joining nature to Scripture and discipline as three witnesses to one law: "Scripture founds the law; Nature joins to attest it; Discipline exacts it... God's is Scripture; God's is Nature; God's is Discipline. Whatever is contrary to these is not God's" (Tertullian, On the Veiling of Virgins 16). St. Paul's argument is richer than any one scheme. However, this serves to show that nature does not stand alone. It attests what Scripture has founded, and in the next essay we will see that the discipline of the Church indeed exacts it.

It will be said, of course, that long hair on women is a mere convention, and that other times and peoples have done otherwise. But this is simply against what the Scripture teaches on the matter, for St. Paul does not appeal to convention here but to nature, and as we argued in the first essay, conventions and customs as such are not authoritative over the precepts of God. If it were mere convention, St. Paul would have no business appealing to nature at all.

St. Chrysostom adds to the force of this verse:

> His constant practice of stating commonly received reasons he adopts also in this place, betaking himself to the common custom, and greatly abashing those who waited to be taught these things from him, which even from men's ordinary practice they might have learned. For such things are not unknown even to Barbarians
> (Chrysostom, Homilies on First Corinthians 26.5)

St. Paul and Tertullian demonstrate, along with a plethora of the Fathers that what is otherwise instructed with doctrines and reasonings is a matter that Nature itself should suffice to inform. In this way, even the heathen nations without the knowledge of Revelation can come to understand the necessity of this practice. And indeed they did. A Lapide gathers the witness of the ancient world:

> We should note (1.), that out of modesty and dignified reserve head-coverings were worn in the time before Christ by the women of Judæa, Troy, Rome, Arabia, and Sparta.
> (Lapide, on 1 Cor 11:10, trans. Cobb)

Plutarch takes it for granted that "it is more usual for women to go forth in public with their heads covered and men with their heads uncovered" (Plutarch, Roman Questions 14, trans. Babbitt). And Tertullian holds up the women of Arabia as a rebuke to the Christian women of his own day:

> Arabia's heathen females will be your judges, who cover not only the head, but the face also, so entirely, that they are content, with one eye free, to enjoy rather half the light than to prostitute the entire face.
> (Tertullian, On the Veiling of Virgins 17)

How shameful it is, then, for those who have received the fullness of Revelation to refuse what even the pagans, without the Gospel, kept by the light of nature alone. What excuse is there for the baptized where there is the double teacher of nature and Writ?

## Ignominy and Glory

St. Paul gives the lesson of nature in two parts, one for the man and one for the woman. For the man, long hair is "an ignominy." St. Thomas explains that the majority of men regard it as degrading "because it makes the man seem feminine" (Aquinas, Commentary on 1 Corinthians 11, lect. 3, n. 619, trans. Larcher). We need not dwell on this, for it is not the prime focus of this essay. It is worth noting, though, that our modern notion of long hair for men is a bit extreme. Kings and nobles of the past often kept their hair longer than the common man, as a sign that they had the leisure to spend upon it, and there is a notion of liberty in longer hair which is fittingly signified in Christ. We may presume that St. Paul means hair that is actually long, as a woman's is long, and not merely hair longer than a soldier's.

For the woman, long hair is "a glory." St. Thomas continues:

> Secondly, he presents that which is on the part of the woman, saying: But if a woman has long hair, it is her glory, because it seems to pertain to her adornment. Hence it says in Song of Songs (7:5): 'Your flowing locks are like purple.' Then he assigns the reason when he says: For her hair is given her for a covering. Consequently, the same reason applies to growing long hair and to wearing an artificial covering: 'Your hair is like a flock of goats' (Song of Songs 4:1).
> (Aquinas, Commentary on 1 Corinthians 11, lect. 3, n. 619, trans. Larcher)

We said in the fourth essay that the woman is the glory of the man, and so in the end her glory goes up to him. But what is spoken of here more plainly is that the woman takes glory in her hair. The brushed, oiled, and cared-for locks are a prestige and a sign of dignity to her, and there is a natural pleasure in the hair which every woman knows, and which St. Thomas notes when he says that "in the majority of cases... women take more pains with their hair than men" (Aquinas, Commentary on 1 Corinthians 11, lect. 2, n. 598, trans. Larcher).

The hair, then, is not only a covering, but also a beauty, and a sensual one. And, lest we be tempted to think that the hair is nothing sexual, we have the words contained in the Canticle of Canticles:

> How beautiful art thou my love, how beautiful art thou! thine eyes as it were of doves, besides that which lieth hid within. Thy hairs as the flocks of goats, which have come up from mount Galaad.
> (Cant 4:1, Douay 1610)

Not only is there beauty found in a woman's hair well kept, but also a fair face and neck, so that the whole head of a woman is beautiful. Here, then, is the double character of the hair. It is a natural covering, given her by God, and yet it is also, by being part of her unique nature as a woman, a sensual characteristic which itself ought to be covered for the sake of sexual modesty, as we treated in the previous essay under the reading of the angels as righteous men. So the hair both covers and is itself in need of covering, and while nature gives her the first covering, it is left to her own will to add the second.

## Nature and Will

This brings us to the heart of the matter, and to the obvious question: if the hair is given her for a covering, why add another? St. Chrysostom poses the question and answers it:

> And if it be given her for a covering, say you, wherefore need she add another covering? That not nature only, but also her own will may have part in her acknowledgment of subjection. For that you ought to be covered nature herself by anticipation enacted a law. Add now, I pray, your own part also, that you may not seem to subvert the very laws of nature; a proof of most insolent rashness, to buffet not only with us, but with nature also.
> (Chrysostom, Homilies on First Corinthians 26.5)

The natural covering provided to the woman by our Lord meant as a sign to her of her nature so she would then put on a covering by her own choice. The natural covering is largely without choice, in a sense, for a woman does not choose to be a woman, nor to have the inclination to keep her hair. The veil, however, is chosen, so that nature gives the sign and the will ratifies it. We may think of it by analogy with grace, which does not destroy nature but builds upon it and perfects it, or by analogy with the symbols we have used before, as the wedding ring which a husband and wife put on does not make the marriage, but confirms and shows forth a union already real. And to transgress this nature is to transgress the dictates of the one who ordained the nature.

Ambrosiaster puts it in a single phrase, that the hair is a token of the veil:

> *Mulier autem si comam habuerit, gloria est illi; quoniam coma pro velamine data est ei. Naturaliter hoc honestum et propemodum decretum vult esse, ut velata mulier satisfaciat, et gratias agat Deo. Coma ergo indicium velaminis est, ut naturae et voluntas addatur.*
> (Ambrosiaster, Commentary on First Corinthians, on 1 Cor 11:15, PL 17)
>
> "But if a woman has long hair, it is a glory to her, since her hair is given her for a covering. He would have this be naturally honorable and as good as decreed, that the woman, being veiled, should make satisfaction and give thanks to God. The hair, then, is a token of the veil, so that will may be added to nature."

Two words in this passage are worth our attention. The veiled woman "makes satisfaction," and she "gives thanks." The satisfaction is for the fall of Eve, who sinned by disobedience, and so the daughter of Eve makes amends, in some small part, by an act of willing obedience. The thanksgiving is that which is characteristic of the Magnificat, for the woman who takes up the veil gives thanks as our Lady gave thanks, that God "hath regarded the humility of his handmaid" (Luke 1:48, Rheims 1582).

Peter Lombard, following Ambrosiaster, adds a phrase which must be handled carefully:

> *Ideo gloria est mulieri, quia naturaliter hoc habet pro velamine. ... Coma enim indicium velaminis est, ideo naturaliter hoc honestum est ut velata sit mulier, et obediat, ut nature voluntas addatur, que ad hoc habet velamen ut ream se cognoscat, et subditam, et ideo gloria est illi, si comam habet, quia naturaliter habet.*
> (Peter Lombard, Collectanea, on 1 Cor 11:15, PL 191, col. 1636B-C)
>
> "It is a glory to the woman for this reason, that she has this by nature for a covering. ... For the hair is a token of the veil; therefore it is naturally honorable that the woman be veiled, and obey, so that will may be added to nature, she who has the covering for this purpose, that she may know herself guilty, and subject; and therefore it is a glory to her if she has long hair, because she has it by nature."

She has the covering "that she may know herself guilty, and subject." This is not a charge against any particular woman, but the guilt of Eve so applied to all her daughters, of which St. Paul writes elsewhere: "And Adam was not seduced: but the woman being seduced, was in prevarication" (1 Tim 2:14, Rheims 1582). The daughters of Eve inherit with her the concupiscence that followed her sin, and the temptation to pride by which she fell, i.e., the grasping after what was not hers. The remedy for pride is humility, and the remedy for guilt is penance, and so the veil is, in a certain sense, a penitential garment, freely taken up. It is worth noting, too, the words spoken to Eve after the Fall: "thou shalt be under thy husband's power, and he shall have dominion over thee" (Gen 3:16, Douay 1609). The Vulgate reads *sub viri potestate eris*, and this is the same word, *potestas*, that St. Paul uses in the tenth verse, as a Lapide noted elsewhere. The same word that St. Paul uses was spoken to Eve in the curse, so that the veil recalls both the order of Eden and its confirmation in the Fall, as we said in the second essay, both remedy and origin.

St. Thomas, for his part, explains how the artificial covering completes the natural:

> In regard to the first it should be noted that nature, which provides the other animals with aids sufficient for life, offers them to man imperfectly, so that through reason, art and use, man with his hands provides those things for himself, as it gave bulls horns for defense; whereas men prepare for themselves arms for defense by reason's direction of the hands. Hence it is that art imitates nature and produces things which nature cannot make. Thus, for the covering of the head, nature gave man hair. But because this covering is not sufficient, man through art prepares for himself another covering. The same explanation is true in regard to the natural covering and the artificial. But it is natural for a woman to have long hair. For she has a natural disposition to this, and further a definite inclination is present in women to take care of their hair. For this is true in the majority of cases that women take more pains with their hair than men. Therefore, it seems to be a condition suitable to women that they use an artificial covering for the head more than men.
> (Aquinas, Commentary on 1 Corinthians 11, lect. 2, n. 598, trans. Larcher)

We are well acquainted with this principle in our own day, perhaps more than any age before us. Our art and technology imitate nature, and yet are in some ways more effective than it, such that we extend or complete (or even now caricature and jade) nature by our art. So the plow carries further what the hand begins, and the house what the cave began, and so too the veil what the hair began, for worthy art does not contradict nature, but carries it further in the same direction to better fit man's need, and one such need is the sign of subjection.

## The First Covering

Where, then, did the covering begin? Tertullian points us back to the garden:

> For both Eve and Adam, when it befell them to be 'wise,' forthwith veiled what they had learnt to know. At all events, with regard to those in whom girlhood has changed (into maturity), their age ought to remember its duties as to nature, so also, to discipline
> (Tertullian, On Prayer 22)

The Scripture tells us what they did:

> And the eyes of them both were opened: and when they perceived themselves to be naked, they sowed together leaves of a fig tree, and made themselves aprons.
> (Gen 3:7, Douay 1609)

The first covering in the Scriptures is the covering of the generative parts, and it comes with the knowledge of shame. And it is God himself who completes it, for "Our Lord God also made for Adam and his wife garments of skins, and clothed them" (Gen 3:21, Douay 1609). Here we have the beginning of the shamefastness spoken of in the third essay. But the natural implication of the first covering reaches further, for if the Fall brought the knowledge of nakedness, it also brought the disorder between the man and the woman, and the veil of the head is the restoration of that subjection which existed before the Fall, the order of Eden recovered under the conditions of exile. As the fig leaves covered the shame of the body, so the veil covers the shame of the disorder, and points back to the order that was lost.

## The Church Keeps the Sign

The Church herself has guarded this natural sign. The Council of Gangra, in the fourth century, condemned those who would cut it off:

> If any woman from pretended asceticism shall cut off her hair, which God gave her as the reminder of her subjection, thus annulling as it were the ordinance of subjection, let her be anathema.
> (Council of Gangra, canon 17)

The hair is "the reminder of her subjection," and to cut it off under pretext of piety is to annul the ordinance. One may ask, then, how this stands with the shorn nuns of whom we spoke in the third essay. A Lapide answers by distinction:

> It may be urged that the Council of Gangra (can. 17) forbids virgins to be shorn under pretext of religion. I reply from Sozomen (lib. iii. c. 13) that this canon does not refer to religious, but to heretical women, who left their husbands and against their will cut off their hair, in the name of religion, and donned man's dress. It is these that the Council excommunicates, as Baronius rightly points out (Annals, vol. iv.). Add to this that religious virgins wear a sacred veil instead of their hair.
> (Lapide, on 1 Cor 11:6, trans. Cobb)

The heretical women cast off both the hair and the subjection it signified. The religious give up the hair but keep the subjection, and wear the willful-sign of subjection in its place, so that the sign is not annulled but transferred, as St. Thomas and St. Jerome showed us in the third essay. And in this they follow our Lady, who, though she was the handmaid of the Lord in the fullest sense, did not set aside the veil.

Since nature's covering is never taken off, neither is the will's assent to it limited to the hour of prayer. Tertullian says it plainly:

> It is incumbent, then, at all times and in every place, to walk mindful of the law, prepared and equipped in readiness to meet every mention of God; who, if He be in the heart, will be recognised as well in the head of females.
> (Tertullian, On the Veiling of Virgins 17)

St. Paul names prayer in the thirteenth verse, as he did in the fifth, for it is there that the lesson of nature is first and most fittingly applied. But it is the same 'especially' which we have met throughout: at all times, and especially at prayer when the mind intentfully approaches God, and most especially at corporate prayer, where the whole Church so approaches God in Word and Flesh. This, too, will be given its fuller treatment in its own essay.

Having appealed to his hearers' judgment and to nature itself, St. Paul now renders them back again to where he started, i.e., to authority: "But if any man seem to be contentious, we have no such custom, nor the Church of God" (1 Cor 11:16, Rheims 1582). This is, in a sense, the weakest of his arguments, not because St. Paul is weak, but because it is the last resort of one who has already given every stronger reason. If the stronger would not work, then perhaps the catchall can save some.
