*The second in a series of essays on the veiling of women, following St. Paul's argument in 1 Corinthians 11. This essay treats verse 3.*

> And I will have you know, that the head of every man, is Christ: and the head of the woman, is the man: and the head of Christ, is God.
> (1 Cor 11:3, Rheims 1582)

The first verse proper to the question of the veil, per se, draws a logical conclusion from Eden. In the beginning, God created man in his own image, and this creation was done through the Word, who was also the image of Adam prefigured. Christ is the pattern on which Adam was made, and in the image of God Christ represents the perfection or summation of Adam. God forms Adam of the clay and inspirates into him, thereby fashioning both the flesh and the spirit in like manner of the Form, the New Adam.

Working backwards, then, God is the head of Christ, who is "inferior to the Father as touching His Manhood" (Athanasian Creed), and Christ glorifies the Father as such. As Form of Adam, Christ is the head of man (and in the order of the resurrection).

After having made Adam, God then took Adam's flesh and fashioned Eve, so Eve is of the flesh of Adam, and thereby is his glory 'flesh of my flesh'. This glory can easily be seen in an analogous way when children show some proficiency, skill, or other remarkable character. The parents can with right glory say ' I am proud of my child.' Why can one be proud of another, considering the work or cause of glory of the other is not the cause of glory directly of the one? The reason in the parents is that of precedence. The child exists efficiently caused by the parents having brought forth, and on top of this the parents have offered their labors to educate and form the child into what they are, thus the success of the child pertains implicitly to the success of the parent. Likewise, we naturally perceive the goods of our own flesh, our efforts indifferent, to pertain to our pleasure and glory, such that a person could glory in their physique apart from any efforts applied to influence it. So, at some times the cause is in the effort and precedence and sometimes it comes by ownership. Both of these reasons apply to Adam, for he both preceded Eve and he also owns her as 'flesh of my flesh.'

Here, then, is the causal linkage that the word 'head' expresses: glory flows from order to order, from God to Christ, from Christ to man, and from man to woman.

## One Word, Three Headships

Peter Lombard breaks apart this word 'head' and its various usages in this passage.

> *scilicet quod Christus est caput omnis viri, id est Dominus, quia cum non esset, per eum creatus est. Caput autem mulieris est vir, quia ex eius costa, sed Dei virtute formata est: et ideo debet subiici viro, de quo per Christum facta est. Caput vero Christi est Deus Pater, quia ab ipso genitus est. Dignum ergo est ut Filii caput Pater dicatur quasi genitor eius. Una ergo dictio diversam habet intelligentiam. Aliter enim dicitur Christus caput viri, aliter vir caput mulieris, aliter Deus caput Christi.*
> (Peter Lombard, Collectanea, on 1 Cor 11:3, PL 191, col. 1629B)
>
> "namely, that Christ is the head of every man, that is, his Lord, because when man did not exist, he was created through Him. But the head of the woman is the man, because she was formed from his rib, though by the power of God: and therefore she ought to be subject to the man, from whom she was made through Christ. But the head of Christ is God the Father, because He was begotten of Him. It is fitting, then, that the Father be called the head of the Son, as His begetter. So one word bears different meanings: for Christ is called head of the man in one way, the man head of the woman in another, and God head of Christ in another."

The three-fold meaning of 'head' reveals the likenesses and distinctions of headships. For example, while man is head of woman, he is so in a much lesser way than the headship of Creator to Creature. The headship of Creator bears infinite distance from the creature, and the creature is entirely dependent on this headship *in loco*, whereas the headship of the man over the woman is much more proximate in nature, and simultaneously much more remote in dependence, as the woman can exist, per se, without the man, while the creature cannot exist, per se, without its creator (though, as St. Paul will add, "neither the man without the woman: nor the woman without the man, in our Lord").

Still, St. Paul flattens the distinction somewhat in his framing, rather relating the likenesses of man, Christ, and Father, as all share the masculine gender, and all are in position of head relative to their counterparts, so to speak. St. Paul's intention cannot be drawn to an utter flattening, as St. Chrysostom guards against:

> But the head of the woman is the man; and the head of Christ is God. Here the heretics rush upon us with a certain declaration of inferiority, which out of these words they contrive against the Son. But they stumble against themselves. For if the man be the head of the woman, and the head be of the same substance with the body, and the head of Christ is God, the Son is of the same substance with the Father.
> (Chrysostom, Homilies on First Corinthians 26.3)

The man, then, though head, is of the same substance as the woman. This should not surprise, considering the woman is formed from the substance of the man via his rib. The question would then remain, of what kind is the man's headship, if not superior according to ontological nature? Again, St. Chrysostom provides an answer:

> For had Paul meant to speak of rule and subjection, as you say, he would not have brought forward the instance of a wife, but rather of a slave and a master. For what if the wife be under subjection to us? It is as a wife, as free, as equal in honor. And the Son also, though He did become obedient to the Father, it was as the Son of God, it was as God.
> (Chrysostom, Homilies on First Corinthians 26.3)

We could look to the reverse- to the New Adam and Eve, for greater context here. The flesh of Christ is the flesh of Mary, Mary could echo Adam in saying 'flesh of my flesh' in a way unique to herself, considering there was no sexual generation to provide for a composite flesh in Christ. As pertains to his humanity, Christ then shares in the substance of Mary in a way that no other son could claim, and the sameness of the flesh He took from her is, in this regard, quite unique as well. Christ rendered himself obedient to Mary, though her Lord and Creator, and Christ rendered himself obedient to God though equal to God, being himself God. The mystery aside, headship does not inherently, necessarily, entail superiority of substance, but an ordering of things otherwise equal under a hierarchy.

We can find analogies of this in our societies, where men, though fundamentally equal to others, rise up in the societal strata to take the mantle of some form of political leadership. These men do not have an inherent transcendence, but simply fulfill within themselves the role for which they were chosen. So man was elected by God to have the primacy, and woman to follow under him. The natural order so defined by the purity of the original contract of order between man and woman was broken in the fall, where Adam rendered himself obedient to the disobedience of Eve rather than correcting and supporting her in all virtue. Chrysostom again chimes in:

> For with us indeed the woman is reasonably subjected to the man: since equality of honor causes contention. And not for this cause only, but by reason also of the deceit which happened in the beginning. Wherefore you see, she was not subjected as soon as she was made; nor, when He brought her to the man, did either she hear any such thing from God, nor did the man say any such word to her: he said indeed that she was bone of his bone, and flesh of his flesh: but of rule or subjection he no where made mention unto her. But when she made an ill use of her privilege and she who had been made a helper was found to be an ensnarer and ruined all, then she is justly told for the future, your turning shall be to your husband.
> (Chrysostom, Homilies on First Corinthians 26.3)

And now the 'why' of the first reason that St. Paul gives pokes through. The woman was made to ideally be equal to man, though under him in order temporal and otherwise so noted previously. Yet, in her fall, she stumbled to a place beneath the man so drastically that she could be likened to a slave, and this sorry state of the banished Eve is recalled in the veil. This does not undo what Chrysostom says, but holds it in tension: in the valley of tears she bears the subjection of the curse, while in Christ she is raised again to the honor of one free and equal, and her voluntary subjection is part of that climb from the 'not-yet' towards the 'already.'

When St. Paul speaks in this verse, he explicates present realities, the 'not-yet' of our current paradigm between Eden and Heaven. The veil then symbolizes and summarizes the headship of man as both remedy and origin, reminding the daughters of Eve of the original state as well as the implications of the stumble; woman is in subjection and man in domination. The original bounds of order which were set forth in the created beginning were made manifest in Providence in the Fall, confirming the hierarchy - the purity of the original state coming to restoration in the Primacy of Christ.

## The Sign of Power

Turning more acutely to the veil as symbol, let us look at St. Thomas's commentary on this chapter:

> A veil put on the head designates the power of another over the head of a person existing in the order of nature. Therefore, the man existing under God should not have a covering over his head to show that he is immediately subject to God; but the woman should wear a covering to show that besides God she is naturally subject to another.
> (Aquinas, Commentary on 1 Corinthians 11, lect. 2, n. 608, trans. Larcher)

Back to the definition of terms, power and subjection correspond to one another. Subject names the one who renders himself under another (sub-iectus, thrown under), whereas power names the principal. Thus, we can use both power and subject with reference to the symbol, depending on the locus of the question: does the sign of the veil refer to the man or to the woman? If to the man, power, if to the woman, subjection, but the object itself remains the same. To the man God gave power over woman, which power was exercised justly in the beginning, unjustly later, and has been under purification since. Returning to St. Thomas:

> In this way it is shown that she is not immediately under God, but is also subjected to man under God. For the veil put on the head signifies this. Hence another translation has it that the woman ought to have power over her head, but the sense is the same. For a veil is a sign of power, according to the Psalm: "Thou didst let men ride over our heads."
> (Aquinas, Commentary on 1 Corinthians 11, lect. 3, n. 612, trans. Larcher)

We have thus far vacillated between the inter-relation between man and woman, but let us further focus on the woman as such. Peter Lombard, reflecting on this chapter states:

> *Omnis autem mulier orans, que inferior est et subiecta viro, quia vir caput est mulieris, orans aut prophetans non velato capite, scilicet sine signo subiectionis quam debet viro, quasi nihil sit medium ad Deum, deturpat caput suum.*
> (Peter Lombard, Collectanea, on 1 Cor 11:5, PL 191, col. 1630C)
>
> "But every woman praying, who is lower and subject to the man, since the man is head of the woman, praying or prophesying with head not veiled, that is, without the sign of the subjection she owes the man, as though there were nothing between her and God, disgraces her head."

The unveiled woman in prayer approaches to pray to God not only without intermediate, but without a demonstration of the intermediate, thereby dishonoring her head, both the man and her very self. This offense arises out of the disorder according to the original state mentioned above, as well as the subjection intensified by the Fall, expounded above.

This intermediary part of the man goes back to Eden. When Adam was given the command against the eating of the Tree of Knowledge, Eve was not present, but she was expected to receive such instruction from Adam. The rendering of the passages as they unfold demonstrates that Adam did not fully relate the command of God in its purity, thereby preparing a way for the Devil to enter in and tempt Eve. Adam failed his job as intermediary. The New Adam is likewise an intermediary between the faithful and the Father, yet he does not fail in this task, being himself God, and this mediation pertains to headship.

In our liturgy, we say 'Praeceptis salutaribus moniti... audemus dicere.' And then the priest, in imitation of the New Adam, begins 'Pater noster, qui es in caelis...' This prayer in union with and vicarious to the Son is made in the framed freedom of the Son, hence 'Our Father', and 'we dare to say' these words in the confidence of the one who taught us to pray. However, this mode of prayer is not confined to this formula merely, but informs all prayers. I.e., we approach God as Father at all times of prayer, and all men praying so approach God as an adopted son of God with the liberty and intimacy so implied. This address to God the Father is then indicative of the direct headship of God over the man, who, as St. Thomas said, is immediately subject to God. Later commentators draw the same line:

> *ratio autem velationis mulierum est, quia subjectae sunt viris, et quia inter Christum et eas mediant viri.*
> (Denis the Carthusian, Enarrationes in Epistolas Pauli, on 1 Cor 11, Opera Omnia vol. 13)
>
> "But the reason for the veiling of women is that they are subject to men, and that men stand between Christ and them."

The woman, too, prays "Our Father" as an adopted son in grace; the veil does not lessen her adoption, but signs the order of nature in which, as St. Paul and St. Augustine teach, she images God in union with the man, not apart from him.

In our previous essay, we raised the question of Our Lady, and we probed her perfection as an *a fortiori* case for the orientation with which all daughters of Mary ought to approach God the Father. Here we add the observation that Mary, though perfect and far higher than us in the order of grace, yet she still enters into dialogue with God through the intermediary Man, Christ. She does not approach God without the veil.

## Rebecca in the Field

Scripture shows this interplay between the sexes in Rebecca, who, upon approaching her future spouse, veils herself in his presence:

> Rebecca also, when she saw Isaac, lighted off the camel, and said to the servant: Who is that man which cometh towards us along the field? And he said to her: The same is my lord. But she quickly taking her cloak, covered herself.
> (Gen 24:64-65, Douay 1609)

The Vulgate names her cloak a pallium: "*At illa tollens cito pallium, operuit se.*"

Tertullian demonstrates in commentary on this passage that the headship of Isaac over Rebecca impels Rebecca to veil herself:

> And Rebecca is example enough for us, who, when her betrothed had been pointed out, veiled herself for marriage merely on recognition of him.
> (Tertullian, On Prayer 22)

On the question of the nature of this veil that Rebecca used, we can turn to Haydock's commentary:

> Cloak, or summer veil, covering the whole body, and having an opening only for the eyes; such as the Eastern ladies use. St. Jerome in Isai. iii, Rebecca does this out of modesty.
> (Haydock, on Gen 24:65)

And St. Jerome:

> *Habent et theristra, quae nos pallia possumus appellare: quo obvoluta est et Rebecca. Et hodie quoque Arabiae et Mesopotamiae operiuntur feminae: quae Hebraice dicuntur ARDIDIM, Graece θέριστρα: ab eo quod in θέρει, hoc est, in aestate et caumate corpora protegant feminarum.*
> (Jerome, Commentary on Isaiah II, on Isa 3:23, PL 24)
>
> "They also have theristra, which we may call pallia, with which Rebecca too was wrapped. And today too the women of Arabia and Mesopotamia are covered with them; in Hebrew they are called ardidim, in Greek theristra, because in theros, that is, in summer and heat, they protect the bodies of women."

This covering of Rebecca, then, was a kind of total covering: the head, the face, the rest of the body. But this does not entirely answer the 'why' she so covered herself. St. Ambrose connects the wearing of the veil to modesty:

> Was it a small sign of modesty that when Rebecca came to wed Isaac, and saw her bridegroom, she took a veil, that she might not be seen before they were united? Certainly the fair virgin feared not for her beauty, but for her modesty.
> (Ambrose, Concerning Virgins III.3.10)

This modesty, though, is interwoven with the question of headship. Rebecca had traveled the whole way from her father's house in the company of Abraham's servant and his men, and she takes up the veil at the moment she hears, "The same is my lord." Her modesty is ordered first to her head, and from that moment she is under the veil.

It is worth noting that Ambrose's context in writing about Rebecca here is a discourse on virgins. However, the veil of the virginal Rebecca is in this context directed to the anticipatory union of her with her spouse, Isaac. This anticipation is noted by a Lapide's coverage:

> *Pallium, ut eo quasi flammeo honestatis et pudoris causa, nova nupta coram sponso suo se tegeret et obnuberet. ... et S. Ambrosius: Rebecca videns Isaac, ait, «Descendit, et caput suum pallio obnubere coepit, docens verecundiam nuptiis praeire debere. Inde enim et nuptiae dictae, quod pudoris gratia puellae se obnuberent. Discite ergo virgines quemadmodum servetis verecundiam, ne intecto capite prodeatis ante extraneos, cum Rebecca jam desponsata designatum maritum aperto capite non putaverit videndum.*
> (Lapide, on Gen 24:65, quoting St. Ambrose)
>
> "Pallium, so that with it, as with a bridal veil, for honor and modesty's sake, the new bride might cover and veil herself before her bridegroom. ... And St. Ambrose: 'Rebecca, seeing Isaac,' he says, 'came down, and began to veil her head with the pallium, teaching that modesty ought to go before marriage. For hence marriage is called nuptiae, because maidens veiled themselves (se obnuberent) for modesty's sake. Learn therefore, virgins, how you may keep modesty, that you go not forth with uncovered head before strangers, since Rebecca, already betrothed, did not think her appointed husband should see her with head uncovered.'"

Here modesty and headship are united in Rebecca's action, and this twin consideration can be drawn forth for all brides and virgins; brides to their husbands and virgins to the Husband of all virgins. The questions of modesty and virginity will be treated elsewhere, but our concern here currently pertains to the question of headships.

To build a counterexample, we could ask why it is that men do not veil themselves before their brides, either actual or anticipated, and examine the inequality of even our modern rituals in Marriage, where the husband approaches unveiled, while the bride remains veiled. This more-or-less undisturbed custom in the matrimonial proceedings elucidates the natural disposition inherent in the relation between the sexes. Sign of headship is therefore so thoroughly ingrained in the cultural sinews of mankind that a veiled groom would strike anyone as absurd. The symbol of headship would 'flow the wrong way', so to speak.

However, it is not merely in the marriage ceremony that the woman is under demonstrable headship, but rather at all times the woman is under headship.

## Under the Cloud

Thus, the woman ought to don the veil at all times as she is not only under the subjection of her husband while at Church, but also outside of the Church building, not only when at prayer, but at all times. Tertullian terms the veil "the burden of her own humility" (De Corona 14) and this fact is even attested to in the Latin "nubere," where the woman comes under the cloud of her husband, which carries both the definition of "to veil" and "to marry."

St. Isidore of Seville records both the bride's name and the matron's mantle:

> *Nuptae dictae, quod vultus suos velent. Translatum nomen a nubibus, quibus tegitur caelum. Unde et nuptiae dicuntur, quod ibi primum nubentium capita velantur. Obnubere enim cooperire est. Cuius contraria innuba, hoc est innupta, quae adhuc vultum suum non velat.*
> (Isidore, Etymologies IX.7.10-11)
>
> "Brides are called nuptae because they veil their faces. The name is borrowed from the clouds (nubes) by which the sky is covered. Hence also a wedding is called nuptiae, because there the heads of those marrying are first veiled. For obnubere means to cover. Its opposite is innuba, that is, unmarried: she who does not yet veil her face."

> *Stola matronale operimentum, quod cooperto capite et scapula a dextro latere in laevum humerum mittitur: stola autem Graece vocatur quod superemittatur. Idem et ricinium Latino nomine appellatum eo quod dimidia eius pars retro reicitur; quod vulgo mavortem dicunt. Vocatum autem mavortem quasi Martem; signum enim maritalis dignitatis et potestatis in eo est. Caput enim mulieris vir est; inde et super caput mulieris est.*
> (Isidore, Etymologies XIX.25.3-4)
>
> "The stola is a matron's covering, which covers the head and shoulder and is thrown from the right side over the left shoulder; it is called stola from the Greek, because it is put on over. The same garment is called by the Latin name ricinium, because half of it is thrown back; commonly it is called mavors. It is called mavors as if from Mars, for in it is the sign of marital dignity and authority. For the man is the head of the woman; therefore it is upon the woman's head."

The first definitive veiling, then, comes at marriage, or even just before it, as we saw with Rebecca. The movement is from innuba to nupta, as the Latin lays it out. Then the woman is under the veil of her husband, under his headship. Thus, it is fitting that the woman be under the cloud not only for one day in the ceremony, but every day of her life, as will be addressed more completely in a coming essay.

The order set down in this verse, then, belongs to creation itself, wounded in the Fall and now being restored in Christ, and the veil is its sign. If the veil signs this order, then to cast it off dishonors the head it signifies. This is the next step of St. Paul's argument, and here we pass the baton to the next essay.
