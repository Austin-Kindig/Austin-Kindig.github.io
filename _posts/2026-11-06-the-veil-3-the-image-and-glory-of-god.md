*The fourth in a series of essays on the veiling of women, following St. Paul's argument in 1 Corinthians 11. This essay treats verses 7 through 9.*

> The man truly ought not to cover his head, because he is the image and glory of God, but the woman is the glory of the man. For the man is not of the woman, but the woman of the man. For the man was not created for the woman, but the woman for the man.
> (1 Cor 11:7-9, Rheims 1582)

As stated in the beginning it is the glorification of the Father that is our end as followers of our Lord. That we seek this glorification of God alone is another well accepted fact that will be passed over in this essay. This glorification is, according to St. Paul in the above passage, the man himself. This is contrasted with the woman who is "the glory of the man." This 'ladder' has been addressed in a previous essay, where we considered how a parent may rightly glory in a child, and Adam in Eve as 'flesh of my flesh.'

In the beginning was the man, and the man was without the woman. God created Man in his image as the lord of creation on the sixth day. Then, after having created him, expressed his will to him, and caused him to sleep, God formed Eve from the side of Adam, so that she is him, but woman, and he can then say 'flesh of my flesh.' This is the ontological backdrop on which St. Paul is here operating, that the woman is not made in the image of God, but rather in the image of the man, and therefore it is in the man that she is the image of God, by him. This sense is carried forth by St. Augustine and St. Thomas as will be demonstrated.

## In the Man

St. Augustine takes up the difficulty directly, for it would seem that St. Paul here contradicts the book of Genesis, wherein it is said that God made man to his image, male and female:

> But we must notice how that which the apostle says, that not the woman but the man is the image of God, is not contrary to that which is written in Genesis, 'God created man: in the image of God created He him; male and female created He them: and He blessed them.' For this text says that human nature itself, which is complete [only] in both sexes, was made in the image of God; and it does not separate the woman from the image of God which it signifies. For after saying that God made man in the image of God, 'He created him,' it says, 'male and female:' or at any rate, punctuating the words otherwise, 'male and female created He them.' How then did the apostle tell us that the man is the image of God, and therefore he is forbidden to cover his head; but that the woman is not so, and therefore is commanded to cover hers? Unless, forsooth, according to that which I have said already, when I was treating of the nature of the human mind, that the woman together with her own husband is the image of God, so that that whole substance may be one image; but when she is referred separately to her quality of help-meet, which regards the woman herself alone, then she is not the image of God; but as regards the man alone, he is the image of God as fully and completely as when the woman too is joined with him in one.
> (Augustine, On the Trinity XII.7.10)

It is worth noting the manner of St. Augustine's answer. He does not say that the woman is simply the image of God in the same manner as the man, which would empty St. Paul's words of their meaning, nor does he say that she is simply cut off from the image, which would contradict Genesis. Rather, the woman together with her husband is the image of God, so that the whole substance is one image, while the man taken alone is the image of God fully, and the woman taken alone, i.e., in her quality of helpmeet, is not. It is in the man, then, that she is the image of God, and this demands of the two an inherent unity, the one flesh of Genesis, without which the woman cannot be rightly understood in her relation to God.

To refine this, we must turn to what is meant by an image at all. St. Thomas states:

> In saying that he is the image of God, the error is excluded of those who say that man is only made to the image of God, but is not the image; the opposite of which the Apostle says here. For they said that the Son alone is image, because it says in Col (1:15): 'He is the image of the invisible God.' Therefore, one must say that man is said to be the image of God and to His image. For he is an imperfect image, but the Son is said to be the image but not to the image, because He is the perfect image. To clarify this it should be noted that two things are generally involved in the notion of an image. First, a likeness, not in just any way, but in the very species of a thing, as a human son is similar to this father. Or in something which is a sign of the species, as the shape, in bodily things. Hence one who draws the shape of a horse is said to depict his image. And this is what Hilary says in the book, On Synods, that an image is an indifferent species. Secondly, origin is required. For one of two men who are similar in species is not the image of the other, unless he sprang from him, as a son from the father. Thirdly, the notion of a perfect image requires equality. Therefore, because man is similar to God in memory, intelligence and will, which pertain to the species of an intellectual nature and he has this from God, he is said to be God's image; but because equality is lacking, he is an imperfect image of God.
> (Aquinas, Commentary on 1 Corinthians 11, lect. 2, n. 604, trans. Larcher)

An image, then, requires likeness and origin. In the likeness of the intellectual nature, i.e., memory, intelligence, and will, the woman shares with the man in the image of God, for she too has a rational soul. But in the order of origin, the woman sprang from the man, and not the man from the woman, and so she is the image of the man in a way that the man is not the image of the woman. St. Thomas draws out this very distinction in his Summa:

> The image of God, in its principal signification, namely the intellectual nature, is found both in man and in woman. Hence after the words, 'To the image of God He created him,' it is added, 'Male and female He created them' (Gn. 1:27). Moreover it is said 'them' in the plural, as Augustine (Gen. ad lit. iii, 22) remarks, lest it should be thought that both sexes were united in one individual. But in a secondary sense the image of God is found in man, and not in woman: for man is the beginning and end of woman; as God is the beginning and end of every creature. So when the Apostle had said that 'man is the image and glory of God, but woman is the glory of man,' he adds his reason for saying this: 'For man is not of woman, but woman of man; and man was not created for woman, but woman for man.'
> (Aquinas, Summa Theologiae I q.93 a.4 ad 1)

In other words, the reason the woman exists is because of the man. This is true both in her material cause, that of the flesh of Adam, and in her final cause, which is to be a helpmate for Adam. Herein lies the key to the whole passage. In the principal sense, i.e., according to the intellectual nature, both are the image of God. In the secondary sense, i.e., according to the order of beginning and end, the man is the image of God, as God is the beginning and end of every creature, and the woman is not. It is this secondary sense which St. Paul has in view, and it is precisely this sense which the veil signifies.

St. Thomas, in his commentary, adds a further consideration, and notes that St. Paul chose his words with care:

> Some object that because the image of God in man is regarded with respect to the spirit, in which there is no difference between male and female, as it says in Gal (3:28). Therefore, there is no more reason why man is called the image of God than a woman is. The answer is that man is here called the image of God in a special way, namely, because man is the principle of his entire race, as God is the principle of the entire universe and because from the side of Christ dying on the cross flowed the sacraments of blood and water, from which the Church has been organized. Furthermore, in regard to what is within, man is more especially called the image of God, inasmuch as reason is more vigorous in him. But it is better to say that the Apostle speaks clearly here. For he said of man that he is the image and glory of God; but he did not say of the woman that she is the image and glory of man, but only that she is the glory of the man. This gives us to understand that it is common to man and woman to be the image of God; but it is immediately characteristic of man to be the glory of God.
> (Aquinas, Commentary on 1 Corinthians 11, lect. 2, n. 607, trans. Larcher)

As Eve was drawn from the side of the sleeping Adam, so the Church was drawn from the side of the New Adam sleeping upon the Cross, and so the man is the principle of his race as Christ is the principle of his Church. The woman, for her part, is from the man and is his glory, and so she is not the glory of God immediately, but through him; and in the secondary sense of the image, as beginning and end, she bears it through him as well.

With these distinctions in hand, we can be careful not to flatten St. Paul's argument, and can rightly understand certain sayings which might at first seem to go further than he does. Ambrosiaster, commenting on the verse that follows, writes:

> *Mulier ergo idcirco debet velare caput; quia non est imago Dei, sed ut ostendatur subiecta.*
> (Ambrosiaster, Commentary on First Corinthians, on 1 Cor 11:10, PL 17)
>
> "Therefore the woman ought to veil her head, because she is not the image of God, but so that she may be shown to be subject."

And Gratian carries a similar teaching into the Decretum:

> *Hec imago Dei est in homine, ut unus factus sit ex quo ceteri oriantur, habens inperium Dei, quasi uicarius eius, quia unius Dei habet imaginem, ideoque mulier non est facta ad Dei imaginem. Sic etenim dicit: 'Et fecit Deus hominem; ad imaginem Dei fecit illum.' Hinc etiam Apostolus: 'Vir quidem,' ait, 'non debet uelare caput, quia imago et gloria Dei est; mulier ideo uelat, quia non est gloria aut imago Dei.'*
> (Gratian, Decretum C.33 q.5 c.13)
>
> "This is the image of God in man, that one was made from whom the rest should arise, having the authority of God as his vicar, because he bears the image of the one God; and therefore woman was not made to the image of God. For so it says: 'And God made man; to the image of God he made him.' Hence also the Apostle: 'The man indeed,' he says, 'ought not to veil his head, because he is the image and glory of God; the woman veils for this reason, that she is not the glory or image of God.'"

By the distinction of St. Thomas these sayings are reconciled. If taken in the secondary sense, as the image of God whereby the man is the one "from whom the rest should arise," they are true, and they say no more than St. Paul says. If taken in the principal sense, as though the woman had no share in the rational nature by which we are like to God, they would contradict Genesis *(and common sense)*, and so they are not to be read in that sense, as St. Augustine shows. Peter Lombard, following St. Augustine, puts the principal sense plainly:

> *a qua imagine femina non separatur, sicut nec a gratia renovationis et reformatione imaginis Dei excluditur. Habet utique mulier mentem suam rationalem secundum quam et ipsa facta est ad imaginem Dei.*
> (Peter Lombard, Collectanea, on 1 Cor 11:7, PL 191, col. 1633A)
>
> "from which image the woman is not separated, just as she is not excluded from the grace of renewal and the reforming of the image of God. The woman certainly has her own rational mind, according to which she too was made to the image of God."

A Lapide, for his part, notes how the woman may be called the image of the man, and how improperly:

> The woman, that is the wife, is the glory of the man, his glorious image, because God formed Eve out of the man, in his likeness, so that the image might represent the man, as a copy the model. This image is seen in the mind and reason, inasmuch as the woman, like the man, is endowed with a rational soul, with intellect, will, memory, liberty, and is, equally with the man, capable of every degree of wisdom, grace, and glory. The woman, therefore, is the image of the man, but only improperly; for the woman, as regards the rational soul, is man's equal, and both man and woman have been made in the image of God...
> (Lapide, on 1 Cor 11:7, trans. Cobb)

We cannot, then, answer away St. Paul's words, as though the woman were in every respect the image of God just as the man is, for then we turn the passage into nothingness and the veil signifies nothing at all along these lines - we would make the Holy Ghost a liar. Neither can we say the woman has no part in the image, for she is capable of every degree of wisdom, grace, and glory. Indeed, we have many women of noble character in the church, influential in piety, austerity, and wisdom in all doctrine. Rather, we should consider the woman in the man, and the relation of the woman to the man.

## Crowned with Glory

If the woman is the glory of the man, the man is the glory of God. St. Thomas distinguishes the senses in which this may be said:

> It should also be noted that the glory of God is spoken of in two ways: in one way the glory by which God is glorious in Himself; this is not how man is God's glory, but rather God is man's glory, according to Ps 3 (v. 3): 'But thou, O Lord, art a shield about me, my glory.' In another way the glory of God is His splendor derived from Him: 'The glory of the Lord filled the tabernacle' (Ex 40:34). This is the way it says here that man is the glory of God, inasmuch as God's splendor shines on man, as it says in Ps 4 (v. 6): 'The light of your countenance has been signed upon us, O Lord.'
> (Aquinas, Commentary on 1 Corinthians 11, lect. 2, n. 605, trans. Larcher)

The man is the glory of God, then, not as though he added anything to God, but as the light reflected from the moon is the glory of the sun. David sings of this when he marvels at the place of man in creation: "Thou hast minished him a little less than Angels; with glory and honour thou hast crowned him: and hast set him over the works of thy hands" (Ps 8:6-7, Douay 1610). He is crowned with glory, and set over the works of God's hands, i.e., made lord of creation on the sixth day.

A Lapide draws the argument of St. Paul into the form of a syllogism:

> This is a hendiadys, for man is the image of the glory of God, or the glorious image of God, in whom the majesty and power of God shine forth most clearly. He is placed on the topmost step in nature, and is as it were God's vicegerent, ruling everything. This is the major of a syllogism of which the minor is: but the glory of God must be manifested, the glory of man hidden. Therefore, since woman is the glory of the man, the man of God, it follows that woman should be veiled, that the man should not.
> (Lapide, on 1 Cor 11:7, trans. Cobb)

And St. Chrysostom gives us an image of the man standing before God in his office:

> This is again another cause. Not only, so he speaks, because he has Christ to be His Head ought he not to cover the head, but because also he rules over the woman. For the ruler when he comes before the king ought to have the symbol of his rule. As therefore no ruler without military girdle and cloak, would venture to appear before him that has the diadem: so neither do thou without the symbols of your rule, (one of which is the not being covered,) pray before God, lest you insult both yourself and Him that has honored you. And the same thing likewise one may say regarding the woman. For to her also is it a reproach, the not having the symbols of her subjection. But the woman is the glory of the man. Therefore the rule of the man is natural.
> (Chrysostom, Homilies on First Corinthians 26.4)

Recall here the analogy of the political leader in the second essay, who, though fundamentally equal to others, takes up the mantle of leadership and simply fulfills within himself the role for which he was chosen. The ruler bears the insignia of his office before the king, not to vaunt himself, but because the office is not his own, and to appear before the king without it would insult both the office and the one who gave the office. We could imagine the disgrace a priest would bring upon himself to appear in clothing out of order with his office, such as shorts instead of the cassock.

But, of whom much is given, much is required. The creation of man as lord of creation implies a responsibility to be a good lord, and here we are brought back to the New Adam, who is the principle of all true lordship and who exercised his own as the suffering servant: "who when he was in the form of God, thought it no robbery, himself to be equal to God: but he exinanited himself, taking the form of a servant, made into the similitude of men, and in shape found as a man. He humbled himself, made obedient unto death: even the death of the cross" (Phil 2:6-8, Rheims 1582). This radical self-emptying for the sake of the other is the very pattern which St. Paul sets out for husbands: "Husbands, love your wives, as Christ also loved the Church, and delivered himself for it" (Eph 5:25, Rheims 1582).

Most men, if they are patriarchally minded, follow instead the pattern of the fallen Adam, seeking to aggrandize and please themselves at the expense of their wives and children, and reducing them, as it were, to slavery. This is the 'domination' of the curse which we treated in the second essay, and not the headship of Eden. On the other side, we have the abandonment of patriarchy and the rise of egalitarianism, wherein there is complete anarchy and the rupture of all sense of order and of service, as the husband becomes effeminate in the sense in which St. Thomas uses the word: "properly speaking an effeminate man is one who withdraws from good on account of sorrow caused by lack of pleasure" (Aquinas, Summa Theologiae II-II q.138 a.1), a cuckold in his own house who will not bear the burden of headship. Both, then, fail to be the glory of God, as the former seizes a glory which is not his own, while the latter refuses that glory which was given to him.

It is worth noting, as an aside, that this same glory is manifest in the very worship of the Church. A Lapide remarks:

> It should be remarked that in the Old Testament the high-priest offered sacrifices with bare feet and covered head, i.e., wearing his mitre (Exod. 28:37), but in the New Testament the priests offer the sacrifice of the Mass with their feet shod and with uncovered head. Epiphanius says (Hæres. 80) that, in the New Testament, Christ, who is our Head, is conspicuous and manifest to us, but was veiled and hidden from the Jews in the Old Law. However, the Apostle is evidently referring here to all men in general, not to the clergy only.
> (Lapide, on 1 Cor 11:7, trans. Cobb)

Christ the Head is now revealed, and so the head of the man, who images Christ, is uncovered before God.

## Flesh of My Flesh

The woman, for her part, is the glory of the man, and St. Paul gives the reason from Genesis:

> Our Lord God also said: It is not good for man to be alone: let us make him a help like unto himself...Our Lord God therefore cast a deep sleep upon Adam: and when he was fast asleep, he took one of his ribs, and filled up flesh for it. And our Lord God built the rib which he took of Adam into a woman, and brought her to Adam. And Adam said: This now is bone of my bones, and flesh of my flesh: she shall be called woman, because she was taken out of man. Wherefore man shall leave his father and mother, and shall cleave to his wife, and they shall be two in one flesh.
> (Gen 2:18, 21-24, Douay 1609)

As is well established, in the beginning our Lord made first Adam and then created Eve from his side as a helpmate for him. In this sense she was made for him, but following the fall the Woman was cursed to become under the domination of Man, therefore it is proper for her to wear a sign of this domination in humility, as we have treated on in previous essays. St. Thomas ties the glory of the man to this very origin:

> Then when he says, but woman, he presents that which is on the part of the woman, saying: But woman is the glory of man, because, as it says in Gen (2:23): 'She shall be called woman, because she was taken out of man.'
> (Aquinas, Commentary on 1 Corinthians 11, lect. 2, n. 606, trans. Larcher)

> In regard to the first it should be noted that, as was stated above, the woman is called the glory of man through something derived. Consequently, to prove this he says: For man in the original condition of things was not made from woman, but woman from man.
> (Aquinas, Commentary on 1 Corinthians 11, lect. 3, n. 610, trans. Larcher)

How, then, is the woman the glory of the man? The man glories in his wife for her many goodly qualities, her beauty, her intelligence, her thrift, and her virtues, and the Wise Man tells us that "A diligent woman is a crown to her husband" (Prov 12:4, Douay 1610). But he glories also in the very having of a wife at all, as God saw that it was not good for the man to be alone and made him a help like unto himself. The wife is thus the glory of the man in all things: in companionship, in the bearing and rearing of children, and in the many difficulties of living in a fallen world, which she shares with him and lightens for him. As the moon gives back the light of the sun, so the woman gives back to the man his own glory, and as our Lady sang, "My Soul doth magnify our Lord" (Luke 1:46, Rheims 1582), so the good wife magnifies her husband, not by adding to him what he lacks in himself, but by showing forth what God has given him in her.

St. Chrysostom adds that to be the image of another is a greater glory than merely to come from him: "But if to be of any one, is a glory to him of whom one is, much more the being an image of him" (Chrysostom, Homilies on First Corinthians 26.5). And he counts the superiorities which St. Paul has so far laid down:

> This is again a second superiority, nay, rather also a third, and a fourth, the first being, that Christ is the head of us, and we of the woman; a second, that we are the glory of God, but the woman of us; a third, that we are not of the woman, but she of us; a fourth, that we are not for her, but she for us.
> (Chrysostom, Homilies on First Corinthians 26.5)

## For the Man

St. Paul's second proof is that the woman was made for the man, and not the man for the woman. St. Thomas explains why the one made for another is the less perfect of the two:

> To understand this it should be noted that the order of the perfect and of the imperfect is such that in one and the same subject the imperfect precedes the perfect in the order of time. For one is a boy, before he is a man. Absolutely speaking, however, the perfect precedes the imperfect in the order of time and of nature. For a boy is produced from the man. This, therefore, is the reason why the woman was produced from the man, because he is more perfect than the woman, which the Apostle proves from the fact that the end is more perfect than that which is for the end; but man is the woman's end. And this is what he says: For man was not created for woman, but woman for the sake of man, as a helper, namely, in reproduction, as the patient is for the sake of the agent and matter for the sake of form: 'It is not good for man to be alone: let us make him a helper like unto him' (Gen 2:18).
> (Aquinas, Commentary on 1 Corinthians 11, lect. 3, n. 611, trans. Larcher)

St. Peter names her "the weaker feminine vessel," and in the same breath commands the husband to honor her "as it were to the coheirs also of the grace of life: that your prayers be not hindered" (1 Pet 3:7, Rheims 1582). The weakness of the woman, then, is given together with her honor, and the one cannot be rightly understood apart from the other.

St. Thomas also distinguishes two kinds of subjection, which brings us back to the distinction between Eden and the curse:

> Subjection is twofold. One is servile, by virtue of which a superior makes use of a subject for his own benefit; and this kind of subjection began after sin. There is another kind of subjection which is called economic or civil, whereby the superior makes use of his subjects for their own benefit and good; and this kind of subjection existed even before sin. For good order would have been wanting in the human family if some were not governed by others wiser than themselves. So by such a kind of subjection woman is naturally subject to man, because in man the discretion of reason predominates.
> (Aquinas, Summa Theologiae I q.92 a.1 ad 2)

The subjection of Eden is ordered to the good of the subject, and it is this which St. Paul grounds in the order of creation, while the servile subjection, the 'domination' of the curse, is what the fallen Adam makes of it. As we saw in the second essay, headship does not entail a superiority of substance, but an ordering of things otherwise equal under a hierarchy, and the one who governs does so for the good of the one governed, as Christ for his Church.

## Glory Under the Veil

Why, then, does the man uncover and the woman cover under these considerations? St. Thomas gives two reasons. The first, that the veil signifies the power of another over the head, we treated in the second essay. The second follows from all that we have said of glory:

> Secondly, to show that the glory of God should not be concealed but revealed; but man's glory is to be concealed. Hence it says in Ps. 113:9: 'Not to us, O Lord, not to us, but to thy name give the glory.'
> (Aquinas, Commentary on 1 Corinthians 11, lect. 2, n. 608, trans. Larcher)

This is the minor premise of a Lapide's syllogism. The man, who is the glory of God, stands uncovered, so that the glory of God may be revealed. The woman, who is the glory of the man, is covered, so that before God the glory of the man may be muted, and the beauty of the woman, which is part of that glory, may be concealed. The veil, then, hides not the worth of the woman, but rather the glory of man in the presence of the glory of God, so that in the assembly of the faithful God alone is glorified: "Not to us, O Lord, not to us."

St. Augustine adds a further, mystical reading of the veil, wherein the man and the woman figure the two parts of the reason within each soul:

> Pray, have faithful women then lost their bodily sex? But because they are there renewed after the image of God, where there is no sex; man is there made after the image of God, where there is no sex, that is, in the spirit of his mind. Why, then, is the man on that account not bound to cover his head, because he is the image and glory of God, while the woman is bound to do so, because she is the glory of the man; as though the woman were not renewed in the spirit of her mind, which spirit is renewed to the knowledge of God after the image of Him who created him? But because she differs from the man in bodily sex, it was possible rightly to represent under her bodily covering that part of the reason which is diverted to the government of temporal things; so that the image of God may remain on that side of the mind of man on which it cleaves to the beholding or the consulting of the eternal reasons of things; and this, it is clear, not men only, but also women have.
> (Augustine, On the Trinity XII.7.12)

St. Thomas explains this reading with a homely example:

> Therefore, the woman is said to have a veil or power over her own head, in order to signify that in regard to dispensing temporal things man should apply a certain restraint, lest he transgress the limits in loving them. This restraint should not be applied to the love of God, since it is commanded in Dt (6:5): 'Thou shalt love the Lord thy God with thy whole heart.' For no limit is placed in regard to loving the end, although one is placed in regard to the means to the end. For a doctor produces as much health as he can, but he does not give as much medicine as he can, but in a definite amount. Thus a man should not have a covering on his head.
> (Aquinas, Commentary on 1 Corinthians 11, lect. 3, n. 614, trans. Larcher)

This is the "hidden sacrament" of which St. Augustine spoke in our previous essay. The veil upon the head of the woman figures the restraint which every soul, male or female, ought to place upon its love of temporal things as it moves towards the Eschaton, while the bare head of the man figures that love of God which ought to know no limit at all. In a certain sense, then, the interplay between man and woman in the created order acts as something of a sacred drama, where the man and the woman play the part of the parts of man, while simultaneously expressing a reality that is manifested between the sexes so considered. It is fitting, therefore, to utilize sacramental language when discussing this topic.

And since the woman is always the glory of the man, whether within the church or without, so at all times she ought to veil, for the veil is the symbol of that order, its confirmation, and its protector. St. Augustine, as we saw, appeals to the nature of the woman from the beginning, and she, being made of the man and for the man, is by her nature to be brought under the veil, which, being a sign of her place before man, is a signification of her origin as related above. And if the woman ought to wear this symbol it is not only inside the church building but outside as well since she should always dress in conformity to her nature. This, too, will be given its fuller treatment in its own essay, but it follows here from the very nature of the woman as St. Paul describes it, for "the rule of the man is natural," and nature does not lapse when one leaves the church building.

Having laid down that the man is the image and glory of God, and the woman the glory of the man, from the man and for the man, St. Paul now draws his conclusion: "Therefore ought the woman to have power upon her head for the Angels" (1 Cor 11:10, Rheims 1582). What this power is, and why the angels should be concerned with it, we take up in the next essay.
