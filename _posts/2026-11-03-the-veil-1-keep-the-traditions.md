*The first in a series of essays on the veiling of women, following St. Paul's argument in 1 Corinthians 11. This essay treats verses 1 and 2.*

Since our *transitus* from the Garden, the nature and expression of humanity has been on a long variegated corridor of culture and custom. In the tower of Babel, we can see this intensified as the ever-evolving tapestry of tongues unravels to this day. As with Babel, these cultural differences have often amounted, due to the nature of variant human expression, to wedges and sources of contention within Adam. One does not need to look into the past at all to see wars, genocides, cultural contentions (or sub-cultural), and our current lexicon is replete with terms like 'racism', 'prejudice', and the like.

Yet, Christ came to undo the tangled mess we have woven with the help of God's abandonment of ourselves to our own devices and corrupted wills. The restoration is upon us, and has been working to roll back the clock towards Eden for 2,000 years. 

We can hear the echo of an ancient disorder: "he shall rule over you." Where there was once a hierarchy of mutual love, as in the Trinity, there has since been the striving of the wife against her husband and the husband against his wife. Neither side has been clean, as men contracted several marriages, and treated their wives as slaves for their own aggrandizement and pleasure, and as women have sought to shake off the mantle of the husband, either becoming independent, or else dividing the one body of the household into a conjoined twin-monster. This fundamental restoration began with Our Lord and his Mother, the New Adam and Eve, and we see the order of the self-emptying servant-lord, giving all of himself to his Beloved, and the perfect handmaid, with her perpetual *Fiat*.

It is to this image we can look for the Christian restorative redefinition of the man-woman relationship of the Eschaton, when Adam can again proclaim with joy, 'truly this is flesh of my flesh and bone of my bone,' and Eve can delight in the service of her husband, trusting that his care for her well-being is greater than hers could be for even herself.

Since the birth of the Church from the side of our Lord, there has been a struggle for the Church Militant to manifest victory over the dominion of Satan until our Lord comes again. This struggle being fought in the hearts of men has led to both periods of general prosperity for Christendom as well as periods of desolation in the eyes of the World. Knowing that "in the end [our Lady's] heart will triumph" and that our Lord has, by his death and resurrection, already defeated the host of Hell, we fight on for the continued glorification of the Father and the salvation of souls.

Yet, we in the world live at the intersection of the old passing ways of the world and the already-but-not-yet of the fullness of time. We must therefore enter the bootcamp of heaven, complete with self-denial, exercises of detachment and the requisite change of heart towards God that comes through chastisement. The pre-purgatorial fires of this life hurt, in other words, and we must submit to the pain for the sake of the promised gain.

Part of this new life in Christ in the not-yet entails the painful rendering of self to other, including within that primitive society of Marriage. Christ handed on to us a new law to guide us on the way to perfection and to correct our erring hearts and minds. We oftentimes refer to these new precepts and admonitions as being part of Apostolic Tradition, holding either the written or spoken deposit as having come directly from God himself despite the fallible human intermediaries by whom we received such things. It is in this vein that St. Paul instructs us, "Therefore brethren stand: and hold the traditions which you have learned, whether it be by word, or by our epistle" (2 Thess 2:14, Rheims 1582), and we have such recourse for our nourishment in all heavenly doctrines. Among these traditions, handed on by word before ever it was written down, is the veiling of women, which Christian women kept until within living memory.

Why did the veil arise? Moderns would seek convenient answers in anthropology and sociology that would tie the veiling of women to the lording oppression of men. Such persons do not have the latitude of imagination to suppose that there can be dominion without domination, and, especially lately in our Western history, we cannot help but perceive things in terms of class struggle and warfare. But, did the veil come down through the ages to us as an artifact of bygone misogyny? I would argue the Christian could not come to such a conclusion while maintaining the veracity of God, the Scriptures, or our Religion. For the sake of brevity, we could rest on the principle that "All Scripture inspired of God, is profitable to teach, to argue, to correct, to instruct in justice" (2 Tim 3:16, Rheims 1582). A method that treats the inspired text as a mere artifact of its age cannot be squared with that principle, and it lays explosives around the very foundations of our Faith.

I became introduced to this practice first through the writings of St. Augustine, and, after a period of discovery in other writings of the Fathers, I came to understand that this issue was one of prime importance. Surely, nothing which God hands to us from the fingers of His apostles is worthless or fated to fade away. Rather, these things are eternal, founded in our common nature and calling, and are all part of the one whole of the Gospel. I have, therefore, become convicted of the need to rediscover in the Catholic world the very ancient and Apostolic practice of veiling. The Catholic tradition of the veil is thus expounded herein and in subsequent essays.

## This Series' Shape

In looking at the issue of the veil in our modern-day context it seems to me that there are several facets or inter-related issues that intersect and wind up on one another when looking at the moral implications of the veil, broadly: (1) the modesty component, broken into both the sexual-sensual and the social parts; (2) the liturgical component, both in the context of the Holy Sacrifice of the Mass and in the general context of prayer; (3) the natural and eschatological.

It is through these different lenses that I will consider the question in this set of essays, mainly taken from Holy Writ and the Fathers, adding to them personal conjecture, and a few odds and ends, to arrive at something of a summation in the end. The conclusion in one sentence would be something like this: *That the veil **must** be worn during times of prayer, most especially during the Sacred Liturgy, and outside of these contexts it is called for as well.*

St. Paul himself, under the inspiration of the Holy Ghost, lays out his argument by way of a progressive set of proofs, rendering a full set of mail against the devil's temptations towards a contrary custom. This set of essays will follow the pattern that St. Paul has thus laid out. Towards the end of this series, a treatment will be made of the question of perpetual veiling, the end of which is to encourage all Christian families to participate in the veil.

## St. Paul's Five Reasons

Hear the words of the great modern commentator, Cornelius a Lapide:

> The Apostle proceeds to deal with the third point put before him, that of the veiling of women; for the Corinthians had asked of S. Paul whether or no women ought to be veiled. He replies that they ought, and especially at the time of public prayer, and he supports his decision by five reasons. (1.) that womanly honour and modesty demand it (vers. 5, 14); (2.) that they are subject to men (vers. 7 et seq.); (3.) that if they go forth with uncovered head they offend the angels (ver. 10); (4.) that nature has given them hair for a covering (ver. 15); (5.) that this is the custom of the Church (ver. 16).
> (Lapide, on 1 Cor 11, argument of the chapter)

Unpacking this we see a number of key items:

1. He begins here, establishing his authority that he has received from God, "as I also of Christ."
2. He continues with an enumeration of the hierarchy: God, Christ, Man, Woman.
3. He then states that man praying covered disgraces his head (Christ) and that woman praying uncovered disgraces her head (man).
4. He goes on to say that woman, if she will not cover her head, might as well just shave her head.
5. From this, he draws the conclusion that if it be shameful to be bald for her, she must cover her head.
6. He then recalls Genesis to show that it is God who is glorified in the showing of the man's head, because he made man, while man is glorified in the woman's head because she was made from his flesh.
7. This he connects to the supernatural realities by mentioning that it is on account of the angels that a woman ought to veil.
8. He points out that woman was made as a helpmate for man, not man for woman.
9. Not to cause division between the sexes, he points out that we must be united: "For as the woman is of the man, so also the man by the woman: but all things of God."
10. He then makes an experiential appeal, "Neither doth nature itself teach you," that the woman's head must be covered because even nature provides a kind of natural cover.
11. Lastly, he says that if anyone disagrees on this issue, there is no such custom in the Church.

His argument, so distilled, reveals the leonine nature of the man, plumbing the depths of appeal to the point of shaming contenders for their unreasonableness (neither doth nature teach) and finally having recourse to apprehend all opponents under the catch-all of obedience to Church common life (no such custom).

We can see somewhat hidden in a Lapide's mapping the contentions of custom in the Corinthian Church. For context, the heathen women of Corinth held a custom of being unveiled, with hair neatly tied up or braided (Thompson, 1988). This was a mark of dignity and grace in their society, and the rule of worldly custom was brought into conflict with Christian custom. Interestingly, we can see that St. Paul's argument is predicated on the supposition that the Church's practice ought to be observed over and above that of the prevailing social custom, which custom would ordinarily constitute the rule of law. Indeed, St. Thomas teaches that "custom has the force of a law, abolishes law, and is the interpreter of law," yet he adds that "no custom can prevail over the Divine or natural laws" (Aquinas, Summa Theologiae I-II, q. 97, a. 3, corpus and ad 1).

I think this reveals the insidiousness of the devil's trickery, who lays out many barriers to right living, obscuring goodness by making it look strange, weird, bad. This elucidates why St. Paul needs to offer such a thorough treatment, to shore up all sides of the question against the buffets of prevailing winds.

## "Be Ye Followers of Me"

> (1) Be ye followers of me, as I also of Christ. (2) And I praise you brethren, that in all things you be mindful of me: and as I have delivered unto you, you keep my precepts.
> (1 Cor 11:1-2, Rheims 1582; margin at "precepts": "In the Greek, Traditions")

Here, St. Paul lays a foundation for his argument, before proceeding to build the edifice proper, compelling the Christian people to obey him as he obeys Christ, identifying his precepts as those of the Deposit which has its source in God. This foundation is sure, and therefore the edifice is sure, and, wrapping up his argument in an appeal to the common life of the Church, he therefore expels all resistance from both the beginning and the end.

Turning again to a Lapide:

> Ver. 1.—Be ye followers of me, even as also I am of Christ. This is a continuation of the preceding chapter. Imitate me, O Corinthians, in that, as I said, I do not seek my own advantage but that of many, that they may be saved; and in this I imitate the zeal of Christ, who sought not His own good but our salvation, and to gain it descended from heaven to earth, took our flesh, toiled, and gave Himself to the death of the Cross.
> (Lapide, on 1 Cor 11:1)

We should take a step back here to examine the question of 'tradition', as Socrates is said to have put it, "the definition of terms is the beginning of wisdom."

## What St. Paul Means by Tradition

By tradition we mean the deposit which the Apostles handed on to the Church, whether by word or by letter, as St. Paul told the Thessalonians. It is not a habit that grows up among a people, but a thing received from Above. The true custom is that of the Apostolic Church. This custom is not opposed to the truth, but flows from it and returns to it. This custom can be called good because it comes by the force of Tradition which is from our Lord himself instead of being the handiwork of St. Paul, or any other. Regarding this custom, our erudite, and thorough, commentator notes:

> As I delivered them to you.—The Greek gives, when translated literally, as even Beza admits, "Ye keep the traditions as I delivered them to you." Hence, since these traditions were not committed to writing by the Apostles, for no previous letter to the Corinthians containing a record of them is extant, it plainly follows that not everything which concerns faith and morals has been written down in Holy Scripture, and that S. Paul and the other Apostles delivered many things by word of mouth. This is even more clearly stated in vers. 23, 34. It is evident, moreover, from the fact that before that had been written which S. Paul here writes about the Eucharist, &c., the Corinthians were bound to obey the precepts respecting them given by Christ and S. Paul, as he says himself in ver. 23. The law preserved in tradition binds equally with the written law. So Chrysostom, Theophylact, and others.
> (Lapide, on 1 Cor 11:2)

We can see here a source of the tension, which then explains the thoroughness of the treatment. St. Paul had already addressed this topic with the Corinthians in verbal form, setting down to writing a lashing so comprehensive no room for contention ought to remain. St. John Chrysostom explicates it thus:

> "That ye remember me in all things, and hold fast the traditions, even as I delivered them to you." It appears then that he used at that time to deliver many things also not in writing, which he shows too in many other places. But at that time he only delivered them, whereas now he adds an explanation of their reason: thus both rendering the one sort, the obedient, more steadfast, and pulling down the others' pride, who oppose themselves.
> (Chrysostom, Homilies on First Corinthians 26.2)

It happens that some are instructed by their holiness, their humility and docility to the Spirit. However, most of us are not so disposed to receive such instruction, and therefore require written support and logical argumentation to arrive at the same conclusions as those who were not burdened by such weaknesses.

St. John continues:

> This is his account of the reason of the thing, and he states it to make the weaker more attentive. He indeed that is faithful, as he ought to be, and steadfast, does not require any reason or cause of those things which are commanded him, but is content with the ordinance alone. But he that is weaker, when he also learns the cause, then both retains what is said with more care and obeys with much readiness.
> (Chrysostom, Homilies on First Corinthians 26.2)

However, one may be tempted to dissociate these foundations from the edifice, and so we can have recourse to Ambrosiaster to protect us from such an error:

> *Postquam mores et conversationem illorum arguit, nunc traditiones vult corrigere. Ideo non hoc confirmat, sed succenset eis; quia cum esset apostolus eorum, immemores erant traditionum eius*
> (Ambrosiaster, on 1 Cor 11:2)
>
> "After rebuking their morals and manner of life, he now wishes to correct their traditions. So he does not confirm this, but is angry with them, because though he was their apostle, they had forgotten his traditions."

And, more explicitly, he names the tradition itself:

> *Hinc est unde superius illis per ironiam succenset, dicens: Laudo quia omnia mea memoria retinetis: et quomodo ubique trado, traditiones meas tenetis [Sup. vers. 2]. Cum enim haec esset traditionis ratio, ut velatae essent mulieres in Ecclesia, illi contra revelatas eas in Ecclesia patiebantur. Ideoque iam non auctoritate traditionis, quam neglexerant, sed per ipsam naturam suadere illis nititur veritatem*
> (Ambrosiaster, on 1 Cor 11:13)
>
> "Hence it is that above he is angry with them by way of irony, saying: I praise you that you keep all my things in memory, and as I deliver everywhere, you hold my traditions. For since the reason of the tradition was this, that women be veiled in church, they on the contrary allowed them to be unveiled in church. And so he now tries to persuade them of the truth, not by the authority of the tradition, which they had neglected, but through nature itself."

*As a brief aside*: Ambrosiaster names the church because it was there that the Corinthian dispute arose and there that the neglect was most visible. He marks the place where the tradition was broken, not the boundary of the tradition itself. Whether St. Paul's reasons carry the veil beyond the church doors is a question the reasons themselves must answer, and this question will be taken up in its own essay.

This reading was not limited to the primitive church, as is testified by the writings of Peter Lombard:

> *ironia est quod sic loquitur eis Apostolus, quia non eius traditionem servabant, nec eius auctoritatem sequebantur. Ac per hoc, quasi noviter tradidit illis*
> (Peter Lombard, Collectanea, on 1 Cor 11:2, PL 191, col. 1629A)
>
> "It is irony that the Apostle speaks to them so, because they were not keeping his tradition, nor following his authority. And for this reason he hands it on to them as if anew."

Chrysostom takes St. Paul's praise as sincere encouragement, while Ambrosiaster and Lombard hear irony in it. Both readings hold, since St. Paul praises their general fidelity while rebuking them on this particular.

Therefore, we can see that these precedent verses offer the Divine authority of the succeeding argumentation, the tradition having been provided by the Holy Ghost, nurtured in the Catholic Church, and delivered finally through the argumentation of St. Paul by the guidance of the same Ghost.

## The Witness of the Church

The testimony of many ages of the life of the Church further supports the force of his precept. Even before the Church, the nations kept some memory of this Order, as a Lapide notes:

> We should note (1.), that out of modesty and dignified reserve head-coverings were worn in the time before Christ by the women of Judæa, Troy, Rome, Arabia, and Sparta. Valerius Maximus (lib. vi. c. 3) relates the severe punishment inflicted by C. Sulpicius on his wife: he divorced her because he had found her out of doors with uncovered head.
> (Lapide, on 1 Cor 11:10)

The Rheims translators, feeling the need in their day to make appeals to the binding force of Tradition against the many sectarian oppositions so levied against the faithful in their days (in our days), further address the topic against the backdrop of divine tradition within their commentary on this chapter:

> Every woman.] What gifts of God soever women have, though supernatural, as some had in the Primitive Church, yet they may not forget their womanly shamefastness, but show themselves subject and modest, and cover their heads with a veil.
> (Rheims New Testament 1582, annotation on 1 Cor 11:5)

It is here worth stepping back to ask oneself in honesty, if the New Eve so subjected herself, though without any speck of sin or the smear of Original Sin, what ought our orientation be on the issue.

## Binding or Passing?

One substantial question here remains: *whether the veil is universally binding, or whether it pertained to a local custom that St. Paul so allowed to lapse?*

As intimated above, we know that there is nothing in the Holy Scriptures which does not belong therein. We also know from Holy Mother Church that the things spoken of in the Scriptures are immutable and true. If these things are the case, I would think the argument that these passages deal with "customs" or "traditions" that apply only contextually is a very shallow one - a non-starter.

If our doctrines and precepts are subject to the vicissitudes of the worldly and changeable, then their force is not in the author of the New Covenant. Their force would come from the correspondence to the fittingness of a people according to place and time. This question has ultimately been answered above, in the treatment of St. Paul's precepts, and it will return in coming essays. Still, it is helpful to build one's walls double-thick, for greater durability against assault.

It would suffice here to ask: *For what reason would the Holy Ghost inspire St. Paul to write these words if they should eventually fall out of relevance?* A portion of the Scriptures according to the abiding and everlasting covenant would be subject to change rather than evocative of the Everlasting. These things just as well could have made their way into one of his non-canonical letters, thereby delivering their desired effect for the people of his charge, rather than being subjected to such questions of temporal relevance. Add to this the observation that if it were a mere custom that St. Paul were speaking of, why not say something more akin to "this is what we do, you should not stop doing this" or "the rest of the Church does this currently, so you should too." Instead, we see a carefully crafted argument deduced from reason, the common nature of man, and Biblical history that demonstrates a moral implication of more durable significance.

Another question so raised here is *whether these precepts pertain merely to times of prayer, or to all times* (perpetual veiling), which treatment will be provided in forthcoming essays. Part of the difficulty of this particular question is that St. Paul's cursive discourse runs through many concurrent streams, as stated above, and as such there is a necessary inter-tangling of the topic therein. We will therefore seek to examine each point individually, so as to determine its relevance to the contexts of our daily lives, before spearheading this question in a treatment of its own.

Having so addressed the foundation, we will turn towards the first stone of St. Paul's edifice in our next essay, that of headship.
