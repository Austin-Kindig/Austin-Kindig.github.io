*The fifth in a series of essays on the veiling of women, following St. Paul's argument in 1 Corinthians 11. This essay treats verses 10 through 12.*

> Therefore ought the woman to have power upon her head for the Angels. But yet neither the man without the woman: nor the woman without the man, in our Lord. For as the woman is of the man, so also the man by the woman: but all things of God.
> (1 Cor 11:10-12, Rheims 1582)

We have arrived now at the summation of the reasons preceding. St. Paul had first laid the foundation of his edifice in sacred tradition, as was established in the first essay, then proceeding to build his edifice on the proofs treated in the three previous essays, and now he comes to place the rafters on the building, beginning with 'therefore.' Mysteriously (this whole discourse has been shrouded in such) St. Paul dressed this passage with '...the angels', which angels and their various interpretations will be handled below. Still, the summation of 'therefore' is not directed towards 'angels' as if the reason is exclusive to them as such, but rather all things point towards them.

This passage will need some of the most comprehensive care and consideration of all of the passages in this chapter, as there are many layers to 'angels' and a dual meaning to 'power.' This dual meaning was latent in previous essays and passages, but we will need to draw this out now in greater detail.

St. Chrysostom reads the 'therefore' in just this way, as gathering up all that has gone before and adding the angels besides:

> For this cause: what cause, tell me? For all these which have been mentioned, says he; or rather not for these only, but also because of the angels. For although thou despise your husband, says he, yet reverence the angels.
> (Chrysostom, Homilies on First Corinthians 26.5)

The angels, then, are both witnesses and a reason. They testify to the whole order of headship, of image and glory, of honor and dishonor which St. Paul has so carefully laid down, and they add to it a further consideration of their own. It is worth noting, too, that from this point St. Paul begins to press his argument with a sharper edge. Having reasoned from tradition, from headship, from shame, and from creation, he now appeals to witnesses who stand above us, and shortly he will appeal to our own judgment and to nature itself, and finally to the custom of the Church, as though to leave the contentious no room in which to stand - a movement from informing the ill-informed to convicting the ill-willed.

Note the force of that last line, that though she despise her husband, she ought yet to reverence the angels. The woman's veil does not depend upon the worthiness of the man who is her head. Chesterton makes a similar point of the soldier:

> The Colonel is not obeyed because he is the best man, but because he is the Colonel.
> (Chesterton, What's Wrong with the World, II.4)

So too the wife is not subject to her husband because he is the wisest or holiest man she knows, for very often he is not, but because he is her husband, and the orientation of her obedience is towards the office itself, and through the office towards God who established it. A poor head does not abolish the order of heads, any more than a poor priest abolishes the priesthood, and the angels, who see the order whole, are not deceived by the failings of the one who holds it.

St. Chrysostom continues:

> It follows that being covered is a mark of subjection and authority. For it induces her to look down and be ashamed and preserve entire her proper virtue. For the virtue and honor of the governed is to abide in his obedience.
> (Chrysostom, Homilies on First Corinthians 26.5)

Here we find again the shamefastness treated in the third essay, the downcast look of one who knows her place and is content to melt into the flesh of her husband, as it were. And here, too, is the honor of the governed, which is to abide in obedience. Obedience is not a dishonor to the one who obeys rightly, but rather is her proper virtue, and the veil is the sign of it, which, as we said in the previous essay, is the symbol of that obedience, its confirmation, and its guard.

## Power upon Her Head

And with this, let us look towards the various renderings of power. Ambrosiaster puts the matter plainly:

> *Propterea debet mulier potestatem habere supra caput etiam propter angelos. Potestatem velamen significavit, angelos episcopos dicit, sicut docetur in Apocalypsi Ioannis.*
> (Ambrosiaster, Commentary on First Corinthians, on 1 Cor 11:10, PL 17)
>
> "Therefore the woman ought to have power over her head, also because of the angels. By 'power' he signified the veil; by 'angels' he means bishops, as is taught in the Apocalypse of John."

The whole chapter bears this out, for St. Paul has been speaking of nothing but the covering of the head since the fourth verse, and it would be strange indeed if, in the very verse where he draws his conclusion, he should suddenly speak of something else.

Yet the word St. Paul chose is not 'veil' but 'power,' and a Lapide explains why:

> There is no good authority for reading 'veil' instead of 'power,' as some do. We should observe: (1.) Power denotes here the authority, right, or rule of the man over the woman, not of the woman herself. The reference is to Gen. iii. 16. (2.) Power, by metonymy, signifies here the symbol of the man's power, the veil which the woman wears on her head to signify her subjection to her husband's power, and to denote that the man, as it were, is enthroned upon and holds dominion over her head. Power here, then, is used with an active meaning with regard to the man, with a passive in regard to the woman; for a veil is worn by one who reverences the power of another. As a bare and unconstrained head is a sign of power and dominion, so when veiled it is a sign that this power of his is as it were veiled, fettered, and subdued to another.
> (Lapide, on 1 Cor 11:10, trans. Cobb)

This is the dual meaning of which we spoke at the outset. The previous essays have been circling around two things without naming them so directly as this verse does: the dominion and headship of the man, and the sign of that headship, which is the veil. St. Paul here gathers both into the one word 'power.' On the part of the man, power is active, i.e., it is the authority given him in the order of creation and confirmed in the curse. On the part of the woman, power is passive, i.e., it is the reverence she bears toward that authority, and the veil is its outward form. Yet, there is an element of the active in the passive for the daughters of the New Eve, who willfully submit themselves.

We may go further and say that this power has a twofold seat in the woman herself. Its spiritual orientation is in the will, as obedience, the inward bending of herself under the man and under God through him. Its physical manifestation is in the veil, the outward sign which shows forth and confirms that inward disposition. We have treated on this principle before, for the whole of the Christian life is a life of signs ordered to spiritual realities, and the veil is no exception. The obedience without the sign is incomplete, and the sign without the obedience is a false sacrament, so to speak, a discord between the inward disposition and the outward sign.

It is worth noting that some moderns would read this verse as though St. Paul meant that the woman ought to have authority over her own head, i.e., that she may do with it what she likes. Such a reading will be given its due in the essay on objections, but it suffices here to say that, set against everything St. Paul has said since the third verse, it makes nonsense of the whole passage. He has spent seven verses establishing that the head of the woman is the man, and it would be a strange conclusion indeed that she is therefore her own head.

Note also what a Lapide says of the man's power when it is set upon the head of his wife: it is "as it were veiled, fettered, and subdued to another." The man sees his own authority, so to speak, upon the head of his wife, and he sees it covered. This intertwines with what we said of glory in the previous essay. The woman is the glory of the man, and she is brought under the man, and so the glory of the man is itself brought under the veil, that God alone may be glorified. The man's dominion is honored and humbled in the same sign, for the very veil which shows that he is her head also shows that his headship is not his own to boast of, but is subject to the One above him.

A Lapide gathers the names which the Fathers and a council gave to this covering:

> Hence Tertullian (de Cor. Mil. c. xiv.) calls this covering worn by women, 'The burden of their humility,' and (de Vel. Virg. c. xvii.) 'their yoke.' S. Chrysostom calls it 'The sign of subjection;' the Council of Gangra (sess. xvii.), 'The memorial of subjection.'
> (Lapide, on 1 Cor 11:10, trans. Cobb)

Of these names, that of 'yoke' calls to mind the words of our Lord:

> Take up my yoke upon you, and learn of me, because I am meek, and humble of heart: and you shall find rest to your souls. For my yoke is sweet, and my burden light.
> (Matt 11:29-30, Rheims 1582)

The yoke of the veil is sweet for the same reason that the yoke of Christ is sweet, because it is borne in humility and in love, and because it is borne together with another.

Here we must look again to our Lady, for it is she who bears humility to its fullness. She is the handmaid of the Lord, yoked eternally to God, and she delights in her humility and in her subjection to the good God. And she is not only signed with subjection, but crowned with it, for St. John beheld her thus:

> And a great sign appeared in heaven; a woman clothed with the sun, and the moon under her feet, and on her head a crown of twelve stars.
> (Apoc 12:1, Rheims 1582)

She who covered her head in humility on earth is veiled in the very firmament of the cosmos, clothed with the sun and crowned with stars. And the blessedness which she foretold, "for behold from henceforth all generations shall call me blessed" (Luke 1:48, Rheims 1582), is up for share in all women who take up this yoke as she did.

Tertullian adds a curious phrase, that the head of the woman is "as already taken possession of by this very thing," i.e., by the veil (Tertullian, De Corona 14). Her head is not her own, for it has been taken possession of. This is akin to 'flesh of my flesh,' for although the wife was not, in her own generation, a part of her husband's flesh as Eve was of Adam, yet she becomes part of his flesh, for "they shall be two in one flesh." And it is worth noting what modern science has shown, that cells from the child remain in the body of the mother for decades after the child is born (Bianchi et al., Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences, 1996). So it is not only in her children, but by them, that the wife becomes 'flesh of my flesh' to her husband, bearing in her own body something of his flesh through their children, and in this we may glimpse something of what St. Paul meant when he said, "Yet she shall be saved by generation of children: if they continue in faith and love and sanctification with sobriety" (1 Tim 2:15, Rheims 1582).

## For the Angels

We come now to the angels, and here the Fathers and doctors give several readings. Some take the angels to be the holy angels of heaven, others righteous men, others the priests and bishops who are called angels of the churches, and still others the fallen angels. It would be easy to set these against one another, as though we must choose one and discard the rest. But St. Paul has dressed his words so that they bear more than one sense, and the readings are not exclusive of one another, but additive, and each lends force to the others. A Lapide judges that the first "is the most literal and pertinent" (Lapide, on 1 Cor 11:10, trans. Cobb), and so we will begin there, but we will find that each of the others has its place.

### The holy angels proper

A Lapide gives the literal sense:

> The literal sense is that women ought to have a covering on the head out of reverence to the angels; not because angels have a body, and can be provoked to lust, as Justin, Clement, and Tertullian thought (this is an error I exposed in the notes to Gen. vi.), but because angels are witnesses of the honest modesty or the immodesty of women, as also of their obedience or disobedience. So Chrysostom, Theophylact, Theodoret, S. Thomas, Anselm.
> (Lapide, on 1 Cor 11:10, trans. Cobb)

Why should the angels concern themselves with a covering of cloth? St. Augustine gives the answer in a single line:

> For a holy and pious meaning is pleasing to the holy angels.
> (Augustine, On the Trinity XII.7.10)

The angels delight in the order of the cosmos, for they themselves are ordered, choir above choir, each in its place, and they delight in signs which are ordered to spiritual truths. This is not only a consequence of the Primacy of Christ which we have spoken of before, wherein the visible carries and guards the invisible, but it follows quite logically from the eschatology we have been building throughout these essays. The order of Eden, wounded in the Fall, is being restored, and its restoration is a thing the angels watch over and rejoice in. St. Paul tells the Hebrews whither we are going:

> But you are come to mount Sion, and the city of the living God, heavenly Jerusalem, and the assembly of many thousand Angels, and the Church of the first-born, which are written in the Heavens, and the judge of all, God: and the spirits of the just made perfect.
> (Heb 12:22-23, Rheims 1582)

The assembly of angels and the Church of the first-born are gathered in the one city, and what we do here is done before them.

Origen saw the same thing in the assembly of the faithful on earth:

> *Ego non ambigo et in coetu nostro adesse angelos, non solum generaliter omni ecclesiae, sed etiam singillatim, de quibus Salvator ait: 'angeli eorum semper vident faciem Patris mei, qui in caelis est.' Duplex hic adest ecclesia, una hominum, altera angelorum. Si quid juxta rationem et juxta scripturarum dicimus voluntatem, laetantur angeli et orant nobiscum. Et quia praesentes angeli sunt in ecclesia, in illa dumtaxat, quae meretur et Christi est, propterea orantibus feminis praecipitur, ut habeant 'super caput velamen propter angelos.'*
> (Origen, Homilies on Luke 23, trans. Jerome, ed. Rauer)
>
> "I do not doubt that angels are present in our assembly too, not only generally in the whole Church, but also individually, those of whom the Saviour says, 'their angels always see the face of my Father, who is in heaven.' There is here present a twofold Church, one of men, the other of angels. If what we say is according to reason and according to the meaning of the Scriptures, the angels rejoice and pray with us. And because angels are present in the church, at least in that church which deserves them and is Christ's, therefore women who pray are commanded to have 'a veil upon the head because of the angels.'"

A twofold Church, one of men and the other of angels, gathered in the one place, and the angels rejoicing and praying with us. This is most of all the case at the Holy Sacrifice, as Peter Lombard writes:

> *Angelici enim spiritus nobis adesse credendi sunt, maxime cum divinis mancipamur obsequiis, id est cum ecclesiam ingressi vel lectionibus sacris aurem accommodamus, vel psalmodie operam damus, vel orationi incumbimus, vel missarum solemnia celebramus. Ideo Apostolus monet mulieres habere velamen propter angelos. Nec dubitari licet, ubi corporis Dominici et sanguinis mysteria geruntur, supernorum civium adesse conventus, qui monumentum, quo venerabile corpus fuerat positum et unde resurgendo abscesserat, tam sedulis excubiis servarunt.*
> (Peter Lombard, Collectanea, on 1 Cor 11:10, PL 191, col. 1635A-B)
>
> "For the angelic spirits are to be believed to be present with us, most of all when we are given over to the divine services, that is, when, having entered the church, we lend our ear to the sacred readings, or give ourselves to the psalmody, or apply ourselves to prayer, or celebrate the solemnities of the Mass. Therefore the Apostle admonishes women to have a veil because of the angels. Nor may it be doubted that where the mysteries of the Lord's body and blood are enacted, a gathering of the citizens above is present, they who kept so watchful a guard over the tomb in which the venerable body had been laid and from which it departed in rising."

The same angels who kept watch at the tomb keep watch at the altar, for it is the same Body. And St. Thomas, taking the angels in this first sense, says the same:

> This can be understood in two ways: in one way about the heavenly angels who are believed to visit congregations of the faithful, especially when the sacred mysteries are celebrated. And therefore at that time women as well as men ought to present themselves honorably and ordinately as reverence to them according to Ps 138 (v. 1): 'Before the angels I sing thy praise.'
> (Aquinas, Commentary on 1 Corinthians 11, lect. 3, n. 613, trans. Larcher)

This is, however, an 'especially' and not an 'only,' and it is the same 'especially' orientation which we found in the fourth verse, i.e., at all times, but most of all at Mass. That the Angels are present for the Holy Sacrifice of the Mass is a fact so well established it does not need treatment, and that the head of the woman must be covered for the Liturgy is therefore well demonstrated as the Apostle enjoins in his epistle with this proof. But the angels do not depart when the Mass is ended. Our Lord says of the little ones, in the very verse Origen cites:

> See that you despise not one of these little ones: for I say to you, that their Angels in Heaven always do see the face of my Father which is in Heaven.
> (Matt 18:10, Rheims 1582)

And the Psalmist sings:

> Because he hath given his Angels charge of thee: that they keep thee in all thy ways. In their hands they shall bear thee: lest perhaps thou knock thy foot against a stone.
> (Ps 90:11-12, Douay 1610)

As is attested to in both Scripture and Tradition the Angels are present to witness the complete intercourse of men in the world, and thus it would follow that if a woman ought to have the veil on account of the Angels present at the Holy Sacrifice she ought to also keep the veil outside of the Church for the same reason.

Clement of Alexandria adds a line which tells us something of what the veil is for in the presence of the angels: "For the real angels in heaven see her though veiled" (Clement of Alexandria, Fragments from the Hypotyposes, on 1 Cor 11:10). The veil does not hide her from the angels. It is rather a sign, and the angels look upon it as the right order of all things between Adam and Eve made visible, the congruence between the sign and the thing signified. Seeing through the veil, then, they behold a woman in her proper place.

### The angels of righteousness

Clement himself, in the same fragment, gives the second reading:

> By the angels he means righteous and virtuous men. Let her be veiled then, that she may not lead them to stumble into fornication. For the real angels in heaven see her though veiled.
> (Clement of Alexandria, Fragments from the Hypotyposes, on 1 Cor 11:10)

For which cause, our Lord instructed us not to even look on a woman with lust lest we be guilty of adultery, though not in deed:

> But I say to you, that whosoever shall look on a woman to lust after her, hath already committed adultery with her in his heart.
> (Matt 5:28, Rheims 1582)

This reading adds to the first and does not displace it. The woman belongs to the man, and so the veil is part of her fidelity, a keeping of herself for him. But the woman belongs also to God, and her integrity belongs to God, to her husband, and to herself, and so there is a component of sensual modesty here which guards that integrity. And there is a component of charity as well, akin to what St. Paul teaches in this same letter concerning meats offered to idols:

> Wherefore if meat scandalize my brother, I will never eat flesh, lest I scandalize my brother.
> (1 Cor 8:13, Rheims 1582)

And again, "Let no man seek his own, but another man's" (1 Cor 10:24, Rheims 1582). The woman veils, then, not only for the order of heads, but for the sake of the weakness or the potential weakness of her brothers, and each of these reasons adds to the force of the others.

### The angels of the churches

The third reading takes the angels to be the priests and bishops, who are called the angels of the churches in the Apocalypse of St. John. Ambrosiaster writes:

> *ut in Ecclesia propter reverentiam episcopalem non habeat caput liberum, sed velamine tectum: nec habeat potestatem loquendi; quia episcopus personam habet Christi. Quasi ergo ante iudicem, sic ante episcopum, quia vicarius Domini est*
> (Ambrosiaster, Commentary on First Corinthians, on 1 Cor 11:10, PL 17)
>
> "so that in the Church, out of reverence for the bishop, she may not have her head free, but covered with a veil, nor have power of speaking, because the bishop bears the person of Christ. As before a judge, then, so before the bishop, because he is the vicar of the Lord."

She stands veiled before the bishop, then, as she would before her Judge. Peter Lombard repeats this reason and adds a second:

> *et hoc propter angelos, id est propter reverentiam sacerdotum, quia nuntii Dei sunt, ut sicut ante iudicem Domini, ita ante vicarium eius sacerdotem sit subiecta viro. Vel propter angelos, scilicet ne sacerdotes in eius faciem inspicientes irritentur ad libidinem.*
> (Peter Lombard, Collectanea, on 1 Cor 11:10, PL 191, col. 1632B)
>
> "and this because of the angels, that is, out of reverence for the priests, since they are the messengers of God, so that as before the Lord's judgment seat, so before his vicar the priest, she may be subject to the man. Or because of the angels, namely, lest the priests, looking upon her face, be stirred to lust."

St. Thomas joins the two:

> Therefore, the woman should always have a covering over her head because of the angels, i.e., the priests, for two reasons: first, as reverence toward them, to which it pertains that women should behave honorably before them. Hence it says in Sir (7:30): 'With all your might love your maker and do not forsake his priests.' Secondly, for their safety, lest the sight of a woman not veiled excite their concupiscence.
> (Aquinas, Commentary on 1 Corinthians 11, lect. 3, n. 613, trans. Larcher)

The reverence owed to the priest is owed to Christ, whose person he bears, and so this reading reaches its height before Christ himself in the Blessed Sacrament, where the priest is only the minister and the Head is truly present. And the second reason of St. Thomas is not idle. A Lapide records that St. Chrysostom himself once had to halt the Holy Sacrifice "through a deacon casting his eyes on a woman" (Lapide, on 1 Cor 11:10, trans. Cobb, citing Metaphrastes). Though it is not said outright, what would there have been for the deacon to behold, had the woman been covered? If it be a matter of modesty before the priests, how is it not a matter of modesty before other men? Are the priests more lusty on account of their not being married? Or are the laymen holier than the priests? If the women must cover before the priests, so they ought to cover before all men.

### The fallen angels

Tertullian gives a fourth reading:

> For indeed it is on account of the angels that he saith women must be veiled, because on account of the daughters of men angels revolted from God.
> (Tertullian, On Prayer 22)

Here it seems as though the angels might lust after an unveiled woman. A Lapide, as we saw, calls this an error, for the angels have no body to be provoked. Yet here, too, a distinction preserves what is true. St. Thomas, recounting St. Augustine's reading of the passage, adds:

> And this on account of the angels, because, as is said in a Gloss: 'Sacred and pious signification is pleasing to the holy angels.' Hence Augustine also says in The City of God, that the demons are attracted by certain sensible things, not as animals to food but as spirits to signs.
> (Aquinas, Commentary on 1 Corinthians 11, lect. 3, n. 614, trans. Larcher)

The fallen angels do not lust after the woman's flesh, but they are drawn to signs, and they are drawn to disorder, for it was by refusing their place that they fell. The veil, then, is a sign which the holy angels love and the fallen angels hate, and it guards the woman not against the desires of the flesh in spirits that have none, but against the malice of those who first broke the order of heaven and then the order of Eden.

If we would have one picture that holds all of these readings together, it is that great sign which St. John saw, the woman clothed with the sun and crowned with stars. Around her is gathered the whole of the angelic order:

> And there was made a great battle in heaven, Michael and his Angels fought with the dragon, and the dragon fought and his Angels.
> (Apoc 12:7, Rheims 1582)

And when the dragon was cast down:

> And after the dragon saw that he was thrown into the earth, he persecuted the woman which brought forth the man-child.
> (Apoc 12:13, Rheims 1582)

The holy angels defend her, the fallen angels pursue her, and the Church on earth, her children, look upon her as their pattern. Every veiled woman stands, in her small way, within that same scene, from the Eden that was lost to the Eschaton that is coming.

## In Our Lord

Having said that the woman is of the man and for the man and under the man, St. Paul at once adds a correction: "But yet neither the man without the woman: nor the woman without the man, in our Lord. For as the woman is of the man, so also the man by the woman: but all things of God." St. Chrysostom explains why:

> Thus, because he had given great superiority to the man, having said that the woman is of him and for him and under him; that he might neither lift up the men more than was due nor depress the women, see how he brings in the correction, saying, Howbeit neither is the man without the woman, nor the woman without the man, in the Lord. Examine not, I pray, says he, the first things only, and that creation. Since if you enquire into what comes after, each one of the two is the cause of the other; or rather not even thus each of the other, but God of all.
> (Chrysostom, Homilies on First Corinthians 26.5)

Every man is the son of a woman. There is an equalizing force in this, for the man who is the head of his wife and daughters is yet under his mother, and this places the whole idea in a kind of tension common in Catholicism, where things that seem opposed are held together in their proper respects. We honor our wives as the mothers of our children, and we honor our mothers as those without whom we could not exist, and in neither case is the order of headship undone, for each honor pertains to the relationship considered. St. Paul is here equalizing. Had he stopped at the ninth verse, he would have given a very standard old-world message, such as any pagan moralist might have given. But this inclusion elevates the whole discourse into something of a mystery, and cuts misogyny proper off at the knees.

We spoke in the previous essay of the false patriarchy of the fallen Adam, who seeks to aggrandize himself at the expense of his wife and children. At the root of that false patriarchy lies misogyny proper, i.e., the hatred of the woman, the desire to put her down because she is a woman. The order St. Paul sets forth is the very opposite of this. It is not born of a desire to put women down, but of a desire to raise them up from where Eve fell, and so its end is positive rather than negative. Nor does it say that all women are beneath all men in every sense absolutely, for the most common case of all, that of a man and his own mother, shows otherwise, as St. Paul here demonstrates.

St. Thomas notes that the four ways in which man has been brought into being end in a mystery:

> For the first production of man took place without man or woman, when 'God formed man from the dust of the earth' (Gen 2:7). The second was from man without the woman, when He formed Eve from Adam's rib, as it says in the same place. But the third is from man and woman, as Abel was born from Adam and Eve, as it says in Gen (4:2). But the fourth was from the woman without the man, as Christ from the virgin, as it says in Gal (4:4): 'God sent forth his Son born of woman.'
> (Aquinas, Commentary on 1 Corinthians 11, lect. 3, n. 616, trans. Larcher)

The whole thing is very Marian, for it is Christ who is by the woman, and in a sense of her, though he remains her Lord. We drew this out in the second essay, where we noted that Mary could say of Christ 'flesh of my flesh' in a way unique to herself. Yet she is veiled before him, despite his being of her. Not even the precedence of Mary according to the flesh nullifies the force of the veil, and in this she is our model. Even though the fourth production departs from the order of nature, it is still ordered according to nature, for the New Eve remains the handmaid of the New Adam who was born of her.

St. Paul calls marriage itself a mystery in his letter to the Ephesians:

> For this cause shall man leave his father and mother: and shall cleave to his wife, and they shall be two in one flesh. This is a great Sacrament. But I speak in Christ and in the Church.
> (Eph 5:31-32, Rheims 1582)

Marriage is a sacred mystery, and so are the things connected to it, the headship, the glory, the veil, for all of these are signs of the union of Christ and his Church, and the sign is not lightly to be set aside when the thing it signifies is so great.

How, then, should the man and the woman take all this? St. Chrysostom answers sternly:

> He said not, of the woman, but he repeats the expression, of the man. For still this particular prerogative remains entire with the man. Yet are not these excellencies the property of the man, but of God. Wherefore also he adds, but all things of God. If therefore all things belong to God, and he commands these things, do thou obey and gainsay not.
> (Chrysostom, Homilies on First Corinthians 26.5)

Peter Lombard answers gently:

> *omnia autem, scilicet et corpus et anima utriusque, ex Deo auctore sunt; hoc ideo dicit ut neque mulier de subiectione doleat, nec vir de exaltatione superbiat.*
> (Peter Lombard, Collectanea, on 1 Cor 11:12, PL 191, col. 1635D)
>
> "all things, namely both the body and the soul of each, are from God as their author; he says this so that neither the woman should grieve over her subjection, nor the man grow proud over his exaltation."

Both of these are true and both are needed, for the order is from God, and so it is to be obeyed and not disputed; and because it is from God, the woman need not grieve under it, nor the man swell with pride above it, for neither has made it, and both are subject to the One who did.

St. Paul has now given his reasons from tradition, from headship, from shame, from creation, and from the angels, and he has guarded the whole with 'in our Lord.' Yet he is not finished, and his tone grows sharper still, as he sometimes does when he has reasoned long with a contentious people. He turns next to his hearers' own judgment, and to nature itself: "Yourselves judge: doth it become a woman not covered to pray unto God? Neither doth nature itself teach you...?" (1 Cor 11:13-14, Rheims 1582). To that appeal we turn in the next essay.
