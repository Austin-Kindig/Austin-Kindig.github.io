## *Ca Éva nos tolleu o Paraís’ e Déus*

God gave Adam seeds and fruit and called it good (Gen 1:29). St. Basil says fasting “was prescribed in Paradise,” since “ye shall not eat” was the first command Adam ever received. He adds that the things “human ingenuity subsequently invented,” such as “the drinking of wine, the slaughter of animals,” were unknown there (*Homily I on Fasting*).

The Fall itself came by eating. Eve saw that the tree was “good for food” and “pleasant to the eyes,” and she took and ate (Gen 3:6). St. Basil says that if Eve had fasted from the tree, we would have no need of the fast now. Abel kept sheep and offered their firstlings and fat to God (Gen 4:2–4), but he had no leave to eat them. Flesh meat only shows up after the Flood (Gen 9:3), wine comes in with Noah (Gen 9:20–21), and the crafts get their start in Cain’s line (Gen 4:17–22). St. Jerome says God allowed flesh after the Flood because He had “taken note of the extreme greediness of men” (*Against Jovinian*). Basil says the same of the Flood: “When hope of human perfection was abandoned, then enjoyment was permitted.” Jerome then says that once Christ came at the end of time, “Omega passed into Alpha and turned the end into the beginning,” and so “nor do we eat flesh.”

There is also cooking considered in itself, as an art of man. St. Clement says our taste is “vitiated by an unhappy art, that of cookery, and the useless art of making pastry.” He mocks people who go to “great trouble to get lampreys in the Straits of Sicily, the eels of the Maeander,” and says such people “live that they may eat,” while the Instructor “enjoins us to eat that we may live” (*Instructor* II). He even concedes that if flesh is to be had, it had better at least be roasted rather than boiled, so it isn’t made delicate. St. Basil likewise tells his flock to give the cook and the pastry chef a rest during the fast, setting down the knives so the table keeps to what the earth gives of itself. St. Gregory the Great lists five ways gluttony tempts us (*Moralia in Job*), and St. Thomas sums them up in the verse *praepropere, laute, nimis, ardenter, studiose*. Gregory gives each its example in Scripture: Jonathan tasting honey before the hour (1 Sam 14:27), Israel craving flesh in the desert (Num 11:4), Sodom’s fullness of bread (Ezek 16:49), Esau’s eagerness for the pottage (Gen 25:30), and the sons of Eli demanding the sacrifice prepared their own way (1 Sam 2:13–16). Two of those five are seeking “costly meats” (*cibus*, not *caro*) and requiring “the food to be daintily cooked” (*Summa* II-II q.148 a.4). So, a pope and a Doctor both count the careful preparation of food for delight as a species of gluttony.

All orthodox Christians agree that sin is in the will and not in matter. Food is still good in itself. The will (and its accidents) is the whole question being raised. Why cook something at all? There are three honest answers. One is health: cooking makes grain digestible and other foods safe, etc. Another is hospitality: we cook to feed our families and honor our guests. (Still, even this has to be tempered, because we do no one a favor by tempting them with sumptuous fare. St. Basil points to Elisha, who kept hospitality with wild greens and a little meal (2 Kgs 4:38–41).)

The third is custom: “it’s how Grandma made it,” or “it’s what the parish serves on the feast.” Keeping a custom need not involve any personal sin. Customs belong to the fallen state of things, which we inherit the way we inherit toil and death, without having chosen them. Even so, every custom of the fine table began with someone going after a pleasure. Past these three, the only reason left is that it tastes better, and that is pleasure sought for its own sake, which brings us back to Ss. Thomas and Gregory.

The same holds for how much we eat. St. Augustine confesses that “what is sufficient for health is too little for pleasure,” and that the snare of concupiscence lies in wait in the very passage from want to satiety, since the passage itself is pleasure (*Confessions*). Health is satisfied before the appetite is, so to seek the pleasure of satiety is to seek pleasure past what reason asks for. Satiety is hardly reached without some transgression, and “too much” is not a function of engorgement only. St. Thomas places gluttony in the concupiscence of the sensitive appetite (*Summa* II-II q.148 a.1 ad 3), so while we need not be insensible to the pleasure of food, the sensitive appetite must always in some sense be held down to observe its proper place as informant of man's reason. This is the old rule of the Fathers that Cassian hands on from Abba Moses: to take food “in such quantity as is required for the support of the flesh, and not for the satisfactory feeling of repletion” (*Conferences* II.22).

The cookery God Himself taught was of another kind. The fine preparations, the grain offerings of fine flour and oil baked in the oven and the pan (Lev 2:1–7) and the showbread set before Him (Lev 24:5–9), went to God, and only His priests ate of them in the holy place. For His people’s own table, He gave the Passover. The lamb was to be eaten neither raw nor “sodden at all with water, but roast with fire” (Ex 12:9), with unleavened bread and bitter herbs, loins girded and staff in hand, in haste (Ex 12:8–11). It is the same preference Clement gives. The art God teaches is modest, hasty, and coarse, the food of a people on their way out of Egypt. Then in the desert, He gave them manna from heaven, and they looked back on the fleshpots of Egypt and grumbled for flesh. David tells us how that turned out: “He gave them their desire, and sent leanness withal into their soul” (Ps 106:15). They had the manna, but they wanted the quail. St. Basil observes that while they were content with manna and water from the rock, “there was not one feeble person among their tribes” (Ps 105:36); once they longed for the fleshpots, they never saw the Promised Land.

We pray for daily bread, and St. Gregory of Nyssa glosses the *Pater Noster* this way: “Give us bread. Not delicacies or riches, nor magnificent purple robes, golden ornaments” (*On the Lord’s Prayer*). St. Basil calls bread the one food fit even for angels, since “man did eat angels’ food” (Ps 78:26), and he sets meat and wine apart from it. Even bread belongs to the exile: “In the sweat of thy face shalt thou eat bread” (Gen 3:19). Christ then takes that bread up and makes it His Body. So even our most basic food is a mercy granted inside a fallen world.

The feasts of Scripture belong to this age. The wine at Cana (Jn 2:1–11), the fatted calf for the prodigal son (Lk 15:23), and the calf Abraham set before the angels (Gen 18:7–8) are real goods; they point to the age to come as through a glass, darkly (1 Cor 13:12). At the Supper of the Lamb (Rev 19:9) we will neither eat flesh, drink wine, nor eat any other food, for that matter, because there the bread and the wine are Christ Himself. St. Basil calls our fasting here the preparation for that table in the Kingdom of Heaven, and he notes that the risen Christ ate to assure us His body was real (Lk 24:43). St. Paul warns against those who despise what God made and command “to abstain from meats, which God hath created to be received with thanksgiving” (1 Tim 4:3). It is not because we are not thankful that we do not eat, but precisely because we are thankful for the honor of adoption (Rom 8:23) that we do not eat.

The kitchen is one of the clearest places to see that we live outside Eden, and fasting and abstinence are how we start walking back. This position is not concerned with defining the letter of the law and its threshold (a silly question for Christians who are not confessors). As St. Paul says, “All things are lawful unto me, but all things are not expedient,” and he goes on, “Meats for the belly, and the belly for meats: but God shall destroy both it and them” (1 Cor 6:12–13). The aim is to mount an offensive to take heaven by force (Mt 11:12). It is the restoration of what it means to be Adam after the degradation of the Fall.

St. Basil makes this active case in the same homily. Since we fell from Paradise by not fasting, he says, “let us, therefore, fast in order that we might return thither.” Fasting is a weapon and a training ground. It armed Moses to climb the smoking mountain and cleansed Elijah to behold the Lord at Horeb. Passivity lets us go with the flow of customs and times, merely accepting what the accumulated sins of the past have handed us, the “vain conversation received by tradition from your fathers” (1 Pet 1:18). That is how the fallen state of things keeps its hold on us, even where no one has sinned personally. The Lord calls us instead to seek out the old paths (Jer 6:16).

## *María britou as pórtas per Ave*

Who better mounts this offensive than Our Lady? At the Annunciation, she said, “Behold the handmaid of the Lord: be it unto me according to thy word” (Lk 1:38). Her *fiat* echoes God’s first creative word, “*Fiat lux*” (Gen 1:3). That *Fiat* is not a passive acceptance, since nothing in God can be passive. It is God’s own activity, both in creating the cosmos and in Our Lady herself, and only by grace could she act together with Him. As Spouse of the Holy Ghost, she is so united to Him that St. Maximilian Kolbe calls the Holy Ghost the uncreated Immaculate Conception and her the created one, speaking of “the union of her very being with the being of the Holy Spirit.” By it, she mounts higher than any creature, receiving the title of Theotokos.

Her sufferings and poverty were more deeds than passions, things willingly and actively suffered. They culminate at the foot of the Cross, where her whole being was poured out in sorrow and com-passion with her own flesh on the Cross: ‘*caro Christi caro Mariae*’ (Pseudo-Augustine). Here, she ate that bread in demure tears, David’s “bread of tears” (Ps 80:5). Her fasting from worldly food comes to its head as she feeds spiritually on the Tree of Life, where Eve had eaten from the tree of knowledge for fleshly considerations. As St. Irenaeus puts it, “the knot of Eve’s disobedience was loosed by the obedience of Mary” (*Against Heresies* III.22).

Those old paths are walked by fasting and prayer, both of which belong to the world to come, where “they shall hunger no more” (Rev 7:16) and God’s adopted sons join the angels who “rest not day and night” from praise before the throne (Rev 4:8). When we choose simple foods and meagre portions over the fleshpots, we take a small step toward the garden and, ultimately, forward toward the Supper of the Lamb.

Fasting at certain times and seasons is a beginning. In its most perfect form, it is fasting from everything that belongs to this age, as far as our spiritual and physical readiness allows. That is how we share in the active self-emptying we see beneath the Tree of Life, with the New Adam and the New Eve. This is principally a spiritual orientation, though, as in a sacrament, the material is congruent with and conformed to the spiritual.
