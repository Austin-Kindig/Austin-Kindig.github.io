*The tenth in a series of essays on the veiling of women, following St. Paul's argument in 1 Corinthians 11. Having gathered the whole case and told how the veil was lost, this essay answers the objections most often raised against it.*

We closed the last essay by observing that the veil fell by force and by fashion, and not by argument, and that nowhere in its history do we find the reasons of St. Paul answered, but only abandoned. Yet there are arguments that could be raised against the veil today, and some of them are raised in good faith by Catholics who love the Church and wish to follow her. It would not be honest to pass over these because the history of the veil's loss does them little credit.

## "St. Paul was speaking to a different culture."

The objection runs thus. St. Paul wrote to a particular city at a particular time, and in that city a woman's uncovered head carried a meaning which it does not carry among us. To impose his instruction on women today is to mistake a local custom for a perpetual law, as if we should still greet one another with a holy kiss or abstain from meat offered to idols. We must read Scripture in its context, and the context here is first-century Corinth.

It is true that St. Paul was writing to the Church at Corinth in particular and within the context of his age, and it is also true that we must regard the cultural contexts when interpreting Scripture. But we must be careful not to become hackers of God's Word. If we ascribe real and eternal doctrines to the realm of long-forgotten culture, we make ourselves unwitting enemies and traitors of our Lord. Every man lives in a culture, and so the question is whether St. Paul's reasons for the veil are drawn from his, which they are not, for had the veil been a matter of Corinthian custom, St. Paul would have argued from Corinthian custom. Instead he argues first headship, "the head of every man, is Christ: and the head of the woman, is the man: and the head of Christ, is God" (1 Cor 11:3, Rheims 1582), from creation, for "the man is not of the woman, but the woman of the man" (1 Cor 11:8, Rheims 1582), from the angels, and from nature itself, none of which belongs to Corinth more than to any other city. St. Chrysostom saw this plainly, and drew the line between what men ordain and what nature teaches:

> For the former indeed were ordained by men, even although God afterwards ratified them: but this by nature, I mean the being covered or uncovered. But when I say Nature, I mean God. For He it is Who created Nature. When therefore you overturn these boundaries, see how great injuries ensue.
> (Chrysostom, Homilies on First Corinthians 26.4)

Moreover, as we saw in the first and seventh essays, the custom of Corinth ran the other way. A Lapide records that the heathen women of the city went uncovered, "and this was regarded at Corinth as a custom honourable, becoming and elegant, and Christian women thought that they ought to retain the custom of their fathers" (Lapide, on 1 Cor 11:3, trans. Cobb). Thus St. Paul reproved the culture of Corinth rather than confirming it. It was not the custom of Corinth that St. Paul defended, but the custom of the churches of God, which he had received with the Apostles from Israel, and it was partly due to his defense of this practice that the custom came to be so widespread and ardent in Christendom. And though the Fathers speak often of custom, none of them treats the veil as a mere custom, to be kept or dropped as the fashions of a region might allow.

The objector may press further, and this is the strongest form of the objection. St. Paul himself, he will say, appealed in the end to custom: "we have no such custom, nor the Church of God" (1 Cor 11:16, Rheims 1582). And the custom of the Church in our day is that women go uncovered. By St. Paul's own test, then, the uncovered head is now the custom of the churches.

Here we must concede what is true in this. St. Thomas teaches that "custom has the force of a law, abolishes law, and is the interpreter of law" (Aquinas, Summa Theologiae I-II q.97 a.3), and the custom of the last decades has done exactly that to the canonical obligation, which no longer binds, as we will consider below. But St. Thomas adds in the same place that the natural and Divine laws "cannot be changed by a custom proceeding from the will of man, but only by Divine authority. Hence it is that no custom can prevail over the Divine or natural laws" (ibid., ad 1). St. Paul gave custom as the third of his supports, after reason, and for the sake of those who would not follow the reasons; as St. Thomas says of this verse, "here he proves by custom what he proved by reasons" (Aquinas, Reportatio on 1 Cor 11:16). Custom upheld the reasons without ever being their source. Thus custom has changed whether the veil binds by positive canonical law, and that change lay within the Church's power; but custom cannot change the reasons St. Paul gave, nor make the uncovered head fitting, and so the contrary custom, though it may be tolerated, is still not to be commended. St. Augustine gives the rule: "reason and truth are to be preferred to custom. But when truth supports custom, nothing should be more strongly maintained" (Augustine, On Baptism, Against the Donatists IV.5.8).

Nor is the practice of a few decades, in one part of the Church, the custom of the Church in St. Paul's sense. The custom of the Church, as we saw in the seventh essay, flows from tradition and is catholic, transcending any certain place or certain time. Against the lapse of our age stand nineteen centuries, East and West, and the many Christians who keep the veil still. And St. Paul's own words already name the custom he refused: "we have no such custom." The custom he would not own was the uncovered head. Indeed, if any cultural sensitivities are to be observed, let us render ourselves to the culture of Christendom rather than the culture of post-Christian modernity.

## "It would be immodest to stand out."

The objection runs thus. Modesty is not only a matter of covering the body; it is also a matter of not drawing attention to oneself. A woman who wears a veil in a parish, or in a street, where no one else does makes herself conspicuous, and invites others to look at her and to think about her. It would seem very much against Christian charity to cause oneself to stand out and apart from the rest of society. To do so amounts to a kind of immodesty, since the one who stands out makes herself a vainglorious display to others.

The objection has real force, and the answer must begin by granting the principle in it. Vanity is a real danger, and a veil can be worn vainly. St. Peter bids women that their trimming be "not outwardly the plaiting of hair, or laying on gold round about, or of putting on vestures: but the man of the heart that is hidden, in the incorruptibility of a quiet and a modest spirit, which is rich in the sight of God" (1 Pet 3:3-4, Rheims 1582). The veil, then, is to be worn plainly, and without display. But immodesty is the drawing of attention to oneself, whereas the veil draws attention, if it draws any, to an order outside the woman, to her head, and through him to Christ and to God, which is the very opposite of vanity.

St. Paul in his own day commanded the women to stand out by veiling themselves against the heathen Corinthians. And we cannot rightly put the virtues associated with blending in above the other dictates of Christian morality. We cannot become drunk for the sake of blending in. We cannot participate in false religious rites. In short, nothing that would be a sin to do individually becomes acceptable in our corporate integration. Moreover, those who would not stand out already stand apart from the world in many other ways, by their modest dress, by the crucifix or medal they wear, by the sign of the cross made over a meal in a public place, and no one calls these immodest, per se.

If it is a good thing to veil, we should do so despite a heathenish custom to the contrary. St. Louis de Montfort, defending the outward signs of devotion to our Lady against those who would keep all devotion hidden, answers the very objection we are considering. He will not let any "worldling or critic intervene to assert that true devotion is essentially in the heart," and he cites our Lord: "Let men see your good works that they may glorify your Father who is in heaven," bidding the faithful perform these acts "without worrying about either the contempt or the approval of men" (Montfort, True Devotion to Mary 226). And if this saint so defended outward signs which pertained to plain devotions, how much more does his argument hold for a sign which St. Paul himself enjoined and enforced according to the various propositions treated in the previous essays? Our Lord said the same: "You are the light of the world. A city cannot be hid, situated on a mountain. Neither do men light a candle and put it under a bushel, but upon a candlestick, that it may shine to all that are in the house" (Matt 5:14-15, Rheims 1582).

Our Lord, the image of Christian perfection, stood out against the world as a sign of contradiction, "set unto the ruin, and unto the resurrection of many in Israel, and for a sign which shall be contradicted" (Luke 2:34, Rheims 1582), and St. Paul preached "Christ crucified, to the Jews certes a scandal, and to the Gentiles, foolishness" (1 Cor 1:23, Rheims 1582). If we are to be imitators of him, we must be willing to be regarded as rigid, antiquated, foolish, and so on, by the heathens and godless men. The fact that we are adept at blending into the current culture should be a conviction to us that we are not emphatically and unapologetically followers of our Lord, but are possibly more akin to St. Peter in the Denial.

## "St. Thomas allows women to go unveiled."

The objection runs thus. St. Thomas himself, the Common Doctor of the Church, says that women may go uncovered where the custom of their country allows it. If the Angelic Doctor made room for the uncovered head, surely we may do the same.

We have treated this passage in the third and seventh essays, and need only recall it. St. Thomas, having quoted St. Augustine that it is unbecoming even for married women to uncover their hair "since the Apostle commands them to cover the head," adds: "Yet in this case some might be excused from sin, when they do this not through vanity but on account of some contrary custom: although such a custom is not to be commended" (Aquinas, Summa Theologiae II-II q.169 a.2). He does not say that there is no sin, but that some might be excused from it, and that only where the uncovering comes from a contrary custom and not from vanity. He speaks of the culpability of the person, and in the same sentence he refuses to commend the custom. Those of us who are not informed by authentic Catholic culture, but by special devotion and by connection to learned men of religion, have little excuse for not following what we have learned. To those of us who have been given more, more will be required.

Nor should we set St. Thomas against the Fathers. The unanimous voice of the Fathers in accordance with the Scriptures cannot be nullified by the opinion of even the Angelic Doctor, though to take his treatment of this issue as an opposition to the doctrine of the Fathers would be a gross overreach. Indeed, he agrees with the force underlying this question considering the non-veiling custom is 'not to be commended.' What is not commendable may only be tolerated as it is itself bad.

## "Hair isn't sexual in our culture."

*A note to the reader: this section touches briefly on matters of marital intimacy.*

The objection runs thus. St. Paul and the Fathers lived in a world where a woman's hair was an object of desire, and where to show it was to invite lust. That is no longer our world. No one today is moved by the sight of a woman's hair, and so the veil, if its purpose is modesty, has lost its purpose.

To be sure, hair is seen as a lesser sexual characteristic than in previous ages. The women of our day are so thoroughly uncovered that the more subtle beauties can seem somewhat trivial in our modern context. But the objection fails on three counts.

First, it is false as a matter of fact. Our culture still treats a woman's hair as one of her chief beauties, and sells it as such. One need only think of the advertisements for shampoo and cosmetics, of the woman in a film who lets down her hair, and of the thing we still call "letting one's hair down." Nor is this merely an impression. Researchers in our own day have found that "long and medium-length hair had a significant positive effect on ratings of women's attractiveness" (Mesko and Bereczkei, "Hairstyle as an Adaptive Means of Displaying Phenotypic Quality," Human Nature 15 [2004]), and another study reports that "women with long and high-quality hair experienced more frequent [marital relations] with their spouse," concluding that a woman's hair "may serve as an important cue for sexual attractiveness" (Cheon, Kim, and Kim, "Wives with Long and High-Quality Hair Have More Frequent Sex," Frontiers in Psychology 14 [2024]). This is borne out by Scripture, for the bridegroom of the Canticle sings, "Thou hast wounded my heart, my sister spouse, thou hast wounded my heart in one of thine eyes, and in one hair of thy neck" (Cant 4:9, Douay 1610).

Second, that we are used to it does not mean that it does not move us. Pope Pius XII named this very argument among the sophisms by which immodesty is defended:

> *I sofismi più insidiosi, che sogliono ripetersi per giustificare l'inverecondia, sembrano essere i medesimi dappertutto. Uno di questi fa leva sull'antico detto « ab assuetis non fit passio »*
> (Pius XII, Address to the First International Congress of High Fashion, 8 November 1957)
>
> "The most insidious sophisms, which are usually repeated to justify immodesty, seem to be the same everywhere. One of these relies on the old saying, 'what one is accustomed to does not stir passion.'"

A thing may be truly stirring while a people has ceased to notice that it is stirred, as we saw of shame in the third essay. And the remedy of the Christian man is not to tell himself that he is no longer moved, but to guard his eyes, as Job did: "I have made a covenant with mine eyes, that I would not so much as think of a virgin" (Job 31:1, Douay 1610). The veil is the woman's half of the same modesty of which Job's covenant is the man's.

Third, and most important, modesty is only one of St. Paul's reasons. Even if a woman's hair stirred no one at all, the reasons from headship, from creation, from the angels, and from nature would remain, and St. Paul calls her hair "a glory for her" (1 Cor 11:15, Rheims 1582), which is the very thing that is guarded. The beauty of woman and the receptivity of man are part of the created order, and the importance of covering our shame was made evident immediately following the fall, when "Our Lord God also made for Adam and his wife garments of skins, and clothed them" (Gen 3:21, Douay 1609). Without scrupulosity or legalism, then, we ought to seek to manifest the fullness of modesty in dress, including the wearing of the veil.

## "Fashions and standards of modesty differ."

The objection runs thus. What counts as modest differs from people to people and from age to age. A dress which would have scandalized our great-grandmothers is thought modest now, and the Church has never imposed one standard of dress on every nation. Why then should the veil, of all things, be fixed for all times and places?

It is true and undeniable that in various parts of the world there are a variety of customs and standards of dress, and Pius XII grants as much: "Certo si danno gradi differenti di pubblica moralità secondo i tempi, le indoli e le condizioni di civiltà dei singoli popoli; ma questo stato di fatto non invalida l'obbligo di tendere all'ideale della perfezione" (Pius XII, Address to the First International Congress of High Fashion), that is, "Certainly there are different degrees of public morality according to the times, the temperaments and the conditions of civilization of individual peoples; but this state of fact does not invalidate the obligation of striving toward the ideal of perfection." Standards differ, then, in degree, yet there are inherent in our nature boundaries that must not be crossed. If this were not the case, it would be a net benefit to society to transcend clothing altogether and revert to a state that recalls the unfallen nature of the Garden.

## "The Church has not spoken definitively."

The objection runs thus. The Church has never defined the veil as a matter of faith. There is no dogma, no anathema, no solemn teaching that women must cover their heads. And the law that once required it at Mass has been abrogated: the Code of 1983, when it took force, abrogated "the Code of Canon Law promulgated in 1917" (Code of Canon Law, can. 6 §1), without repeating the canon on head coverings. Where the Church has not bound, we are free.

The objector is right that there is no definition, and nothing in these essays should be read as claiming otherwise. We may not say that the Church teaches the veil as a doctrine of faith. He is right, too, that the canonical obligation has lapsed. But a definition is not the only way in which the Church knows what is true. What was latent before heresies and scandals arose became manifest and defined only afterwards, and those who came before got along well enough without the more crystallized and detailed teaching that controversy later required. St. Augustine says as much: "While the hot restlessness of heretics stirs questions about many articles of the Catholic faith," "the necessity of defending them forces us both to investigate them more accurately, to understand them more clearly, and to proclaim them more earnestly" (Augustine, City of God XVI.2). The Church has not defined the veil because, for nineteen centuries, no major dispute in the Church arose. It was simply kept, at prayer and otherwise, as we saw in the eighth essay. Nor were the pastors silent when it was questioned, for already in 1582 the Rheims annotators could appeal to "the custom of fifteen hundred years, and the decrees of many hundred Pastors" (Rheims New Testament 1582, annotation on 1 Cor 11:16), though our own age may be more silent. Even in 1969, when the press reported that the rule had been changed, the secretary of the Congregation for Divine Worship answered, "The rule has not been changed. It is a matter of general discipline. It began as a custom in the time of St. Paul and was later incorporated into canon law" (UPI, The Atlanta Journal, June 21, 1969).

What the Code of 1983 lifted, then, was the legal due, the obligation which the Church's law had placed upon the practice. It did not lift, nor could it, the reasons St. Paul gave, which are the ground of the moral due of which we spoke in the seventh essay. Regardless of the historical movements against this practice, we have found provision against the attack in the mind of God, who enshrined the practice in the Scriptures. We would be silly men to require decrees of and anathemas regarding the veil. Our Lord has provided us with sufficient means to come to knowledge about this practice without such things.

## "The Church says the veil no longer binds."

The objection runs thus. The Congregation for the Doctrine of the Faith, in its declaration *Inter insigniores* of 1976, spoke of the veil directly, and said that St. Paul's ordinances on women, "probably inspired by the customs of the period, concern scarcely more than disciplinary practices of minor importance, such as the obligation imposed upon women to wear a veil on their head (1 Cor 11:2-16); such requirements no longer have a normative value" (CDF, Inter insigniores 4). The Church has spoken, and she has said that the veil is a custom of the period and binds no longer.

This is the most serious of the objections, for it comes from the Church's own documents, and it deserves a careful answer.

First, it must be noted that the remark is a passing one. It occurs in a declaration on the admission of women to the ministerial priesthood, in answer to a different objection, and the veil is mentioned only as an example. Second, its historical judgment is hedged by its own wording: the ordinances were "probably inspired by the customs of the period." That is a conjecture about history, offered as probable, and not a teaching. Third, its claim is limited by its own words to the obligation. It speaks of "disciplinary practices," of "the obligation imposed," and says that "such requirements no longer have a normative value."

This must be answered by distinction. St. Paul praised the Corinthians because "as I have delivered unto you, you keep my precepts" (1 Cor 11:2, Rheims 1582), and his precept holds two things together, the obligation, which is positive law, and the instruction, which is the teaching of what the sign means and why it is fitting. The obligation lies within the Church's purview. She may bind it and she may relax it, and in our day she has relaxed it. The principles St. Paul gives, headship, creation, the angels, and nature, are not hers to change, and the passage does not touch them. Indeed, in the very next sentence the declaration grounds St. Paul's prohibition on women's teaching in the assembly in "the divine plan of creation (1 Cor 11:7; Gen 2:18-24)," citing the verse on which St. Paul grounds the veil: "The man truly ought not to cover his head, because he is the image and glory of God, but the woman is the glory of the man" (1 Cor 11:7, Rheims 1582). The Congregation grants that the teaching of this chapter rests on creation, and lifts only the positive obligation of the sign. This we may accept in full, for the law is relaxed, but the basis that first called for it remains.

What, then, becomes of the veil when its obligation is lifted? Not all of it rested on positive law. St. Paul appeals also to nature, which St. Thomas reads as the natural inclination of women to keep the covering of their hair, a work of God and so of the natural law, and he adds that "the same reason applies to growing long hair and to wearing an artificial covering" (Aquinas, Commentary on 1 Corinthians 11, lect. 3, n. 619, trans. Larcher). What nature teaches remains commanded: that a woman keep the covering nature gave her, that she be modest, and that she honor her head. But the cloth veil as a fixed rule is what St. Thomas calls a determination, and such laws "have no other force than that of human law" (Aquinas, Summa Theologiae I-II q.95 a.2). This is the same pattern St. Thomas finds in the Sabbath, whose moral part, the giving of time to God, remains, while its determination to a certain day was set by "the institution of the Church and the custom of Christian people" (Aquinas, Summa Theologiae II-II q.122 a.4 ad 4). And it is unlike the Apostles' ban on blood in the fifteenth chapter of Acts, which lapsed, as St. Thomas says, because "with the lapse of the cause, the effect lapsed also" (Aquinas, Summa Theologiae I-II q.103 a.4 ad 3). The cause of that ban was the union of Jew and Gentile in one Church, and it passed; the causes of the veil have not. It is this part, then, the determination, that passes from commandment to counsel.

St. Thomas explains the difference between the two: "a commandment implies obligation, whereas a counsel is left to the option of the one to whom it is given. Consequently in the New Law, which is the law of liberty, counsels are added to the commandments" (Aquinas, Summa Theologiae I-II q.108 a.4). A counsel is not an indifferent thing. It is, in the terms we took from St. Thomas in the seventh essay, the moral due of the second degree, that which "conduces to greater rectitude, although without it rectitude may be ensured" (Aquinas, Summa Theologiae II-II q.80 a.1). The woman who veils today does so freely, as the better thing, for the same reasons St. Paul gave. Yet in this sense she may truly be said to need it. St. Thomas says that "a thing is said to be necessary for a certain end in two ways. First, when the end cannot be without it; as food is necessary for the preservation of human life. Secondly, when the end is attained better and more conveniently, as a horse is necessary for a journey" (Aquinas, Summa Theologiae III q.1 a.2). It is in this second way that he calls the Incarnation itself necessary, and it is in this way that the veil is necessary to a woman, for by it the order of creation is shown and the angels honored better and more fittingly.

Nor should a counsel be thought of as no obligation at all. St. Thomas, in the very place where he distinguishes the two degrees of moral due, says that "a due implies necessity," so that the second degree is still a due, though a lesser one (Aquinas, Summa Theologiae II-II q.80 a.1). And of the counsels he says that one "sins not by omitting them but by contempt of them" (Aquinas, Summa Theologiae II-II q.186 a.2). Thus the woman who has understood St. Paul's reasons must still ask herself, **'why not?'** And if her only answer is that she despises the sign, or the order it signifies, then the counsel has become, for her, a matter of sin. There may be good reasons to wait, such as her husband's command, or charity in a particular circumstance, or that she is only beginning to understand the reasons, and we will take these up in the next essay. But all of these put off the counsel without despising it.

As to "minor importance," St. Chrysostom:

> And tell me not this, that the error is but small...though it were small, it became great because of the greatness of the things whereof it is a sign.
> (Chrysostom, Homilies on First Corinthians 26.4)

As a sign it is great by what it signifies. St. Paul bound the woman to the veil, as St. Thomas says, by a three-ply cord, of reason, of custom, and of command (Aquinas, Commentary on 1 Corinthians 11, lect. 4, n. 622, trans. Larcher). The command has been loosed by lawful authority, and the custom is frayed in our corner of the Church, though whole across her history. Yet the strand of reason remains, and it is the one no authority can loose.

*As an aside*, in reflecting on the example St. Thomas gives of the Sabbath, how congruent would we find a determination that moved the Lord's day back to Saturday, for instance to draw into greater concourse with the Jews? I would think many Christians would see this as a rupture, despite its being within the Church's power to enact such a change.

## "It is only for Mass."

The objection runs thus. St. Paul speaks of women "praying or prophesying," and the Church's law required the veil at the sacred rites. Whatever we owe at Mass, the veil does not belong to the outside.

This was the whole question of the eighth essay, and we need only recall its answer. St. Chrysostom reads St. Paul's own argument as reaching beyond the hour of prayer: the man is to be uncovered only when he prays, but "the woman he commands to be at all times covered," for "if to be shaven is always dishonorable, it is plain too that being uncovered is always a reproach," and St. Paul "signifies that not at the time of prayer only but also continually, she ought to be covered" (Chrysostom, Homilies on First Corinthians 26.4). And as we saw with Denis the Carthusian, the man's uncovering "should be understood of public and solemn prayer," whereas the reason for the woman's veil is her place in the order, so that the one sign is bound to an act and the other to a state, and a state is not put off when the act is finished. The woman is to be covered at all times, and especially at prayer.

## "It demeans women."

The objection runs thus, and it is often put more bluntly than this. A woman of our day may read of the "famous misogynist Paul of Tarsus," as one popular writer calls him, and conclude that a sign of subjection is a sign that she is worth less than a man. To ask her to wear it is to ask her to agree to her own lowering.

The objection assumes that to be under another is to be lesser in ultimate worth. But St. Paul's own verse refutes this: "the head of every man, is Christ: and the head of the woman, is the man: and the head of Christ, is God" (1 Cor 11:3, Rheims 1582). The Son is under the Father as his head, and no orthodox Catholic thinks the Son is worth less than the Father. Our Lord himself, as a boy, went down to Nazareth "and was subject to them" (Luke 2:51, Rheims 1582), to Mary and to Joseph, and was not made less by it. Indeed, inferiority in some respect is not in itself a bad thing. The Creed of St. Athanasius confesses Christ "Equal to the Father as touching His Godhead, and inferior to the Father as touching His Manhood." St. Thomas teaches that even in the state of innocence "there would have been some inequality" among men, "so that, however, in those who were thus surpassed, there would have been no defect or fault either in soul or body" (Aquinas, Summa Theologiae I q.96 a.3). Order is not injustice, and one placed lower in an order of love is not thereby despised.

We treated this at length in the second and fifth essays. St. Paul himself guards against the misreading: "But yet neither the man without the woman; nor the woman without the man, in our Lord" (1 Cor 11:11, Rheims 1582), and elsewhere, "there is not male nor female. For all you are one in Christ Jesus" (Gal 3:28, Rheims 1582). The headship of the husband is a headship of self-giving: "Husbands, love your wives, as Christ also loved the Church, and delivered himself for it" (Eph 5:25, Rheims 1582). And the highest of all creatures is a woman who called herself a handmaid: "Behold the handmaid of our Lord, be it done to me according to thy word" (Luke 1:38, Rheims 1582). If the sign of subjection demeaned woman, it would demean the Queen of Heaven, and no Catholic can say that in good conscience.

## The End of the Chain

With this we have come to the end of the argument proper. Over nine essays we have followed St. Paul's reasons, the tradition he delivered, the order of headship, the shame of the uncovered head, the order of creation, the angels, and the teaching of nature, and we have seen the Church keep them by her custom, the Fathers carry them from the church into the street, and our own age lose the veil by fashion and by force, and not by argument.

And in this essay we have heard the arguments that remain, and found that each of them, put fairly, leaves St. Paul's reasons standing. The veil was spoken of in a culture, but not drawn from it. It may be worn without vanity, and is no more immodest than any other sign of the faith. St. Thomas excused some who went without it, but would not commend their custom. Our age has grown used to the beauty of a woman's hair, but has not ceased to be moved by it. Standards of dress differ in degree, but not the boundaries set in our nature. The Church has not defined the veil, because no one in the Church disputed it, and the law which bound it has been relaxed, as was within her power, while the reasons which called for it remain, so that it passes from commandment to counsel. It belongs to all of a woman's life, and especially to her prayer. It does not demean her, for the Son is subject to the Father and the handmaid is Queen.

It remains only to ask what a woman who has followed this argument should do, and how. That is the matter of the next essay.
