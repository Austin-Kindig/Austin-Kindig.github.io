*The seventh in a series of essays on the veiling of women, following St. Paul's argument in 1 Corinthians 11. This essay treats verse 16.*

> But if any man seem to be contentious, we have no such custom, nor the Church of God.
> (1 Cor 11:16, Rheims 1582)

St. Paul has built his edifice from the foundations up, and armed it, and now he raises the ramparts around it. The matter of honor or dishonor could be argued to be in relation to custom. Though this matter of custom has been touched on already, its significance regarding the morality of the veil considering the mores, etc. calls for a fuller treatment in this essay. It seems as though the Holy Ghost, seeing the turbulence of the ages as well as the trampling and neglect of these doctrines of St. Paul, held provision in the text to address the issue of custom.

We said at the close of the previous essay that this is, in a sense, the weakest of St. Paul's arguments. It is not weak in the sense of being bad, but in the sense that it pertains to an outside reality, i.e., to the one in authority. The reasons from headship, from shame, from creation, from the angels, and from nature all show why the thing is so, and the mind can follow them to their end. The appeal to custom, by contrast, has more apparent arbitrariness about it, as when a father, having explained himself to a stubborn child, finally says, 'because I said so.' Yet it is no less sufficient for that, and St. Thomas, in his Reportatio, tells us why St. Paul reaches for it here:

> *Hic probat per consuetudinem quod probavit per rationes, et ratio huius est quia illae rationes possent alicui videri non multum efficaces cum sint mysticae et figuratae. Unde ne aliquis contentiose resisteret, intendit alia via convincere eum.*
> (Aquinas, Reportatio on 1 Cor 11:16)
>
> "Here he proves by custom what he proved by reasons, and the reason for this is that those reasons might seem to someone not very effective, since they are mystical and figurative. Hence, lest anyone resist contentiously, he intends to convince him by another way."

The reasons are deep, and since some will not follow them to the bottom, St. Paul gives to these a plainer word, which is the word of authority.

## The Contentious

Who, then, are the contentious? St. Thomas answers in his commentary:

> Then when he says, If anyone, he silences the impudent hearers, saying: If anyone is disposed to be contentious and not acquiesce in the above reason but would attack the truth with confident clamoring, which pertains to contentiousness, as Ambrose says, contrary to Jb (6:29): 'Respond, I pray, without contentiousness'; (Pr 20:3): 'It is an honor for a man to keep aloof from strife.' Let this suffice, then, to silence them that we Jews believing in Christ do not have such a practice, namely, of women praying with their heads uncovering, nor do the churches of God dispersed among the Gentiles. Hence if there were no reason, this alone should suffice, that no one should act against the common custom of the Church: 'He makes those of one outlook to dwell in their house' (Ps 68:7). Hence Augustine says: 'In all cases in which Sacred Scripture has defined nothing definite, the customs of the people of God and the edicts of superiors must be regarded as the law.'
> (Aquinas, Commentary on 1 Corinthians 11, lect. 3, n. 620, trans. Larcher)

The contentious are those who disagree, who will not acquiesce in the reason but mount an attack on truth. Here a distinction must be made. The one who has never heard these things is not contentious merely for not having heard them, and if he hears and then acquiesces, he is not contentious at all, but docile. It is the one who has heard and yet resists who is contentious, and it is to him that St. Paul addresses this last word.

## Two Customs

There were two customs at Corinth, as we saw in the first essay. The heathen women of the city held a custom of going with heads uncovered, and a Lapide notes that "this was regarded at Corinth as a custom honourable, becoming, and elegant, and Christian women thought that they ought to retain the custom of their fathers" (Lapide, on 1 Cor 11:3, trans. Cobb). Against this custom of the city St. Paul sets the custom of the Church. The fact that St. Paul needed to reproach the Corinthians for going about without heads covered is a testament to the fact that they had no such custom among themselves. His approach is not that of confirming custom but of giving reproach. As is attested to by historians, and even detractors of this practice, the Corinthian heathen women held a custom of not veiling, a custom that St. Paul was discouraging in the faithful.

What, then, is the custom of the Church, and how does it differ from the customs of men? The custom of the Church flows from tradition, from that deposit which St. Paul praised the Corinthians for keeping in the second verse. And because it flows from tradition, it transcends any certain place or certain time, being fully catholic. The customs of men belong to a people or an age, and pass away with them, whereas the custom of the Church belongs to the whole Church, in every place and in every time. Were it otherwise, St. Paul would have lacked all foresight, for he would have set as the bow on top of his argument, so to speak, an appeal which nullified the several verses before it, as though to say, 'all these reasons from creation and nature and the angels hold, but only so long as the custom of the day agrees.'

## Fifteen Hundred Years

The translators of the Rheims New Testament saw this clearly, and their note on this verse reads as though it had been written for our own day:

> If women or other, to defend their disorder and malapertness, dispute or allege Scriptures and reasons, or require causes of their preachers why and by what authority they should be thus restrained in things indifferent, make them no other answer but this, This is the custom of the Church, this is our custom. Which is a goodly rule to repress the sauciness of contentious janglers, which being out of all modesty and reason, never want words and replies against the Church. Which Church if it could then by prescription of twenty or thirty years, and by the authority of one or two of their first preachers, stop the mouths of the seditious: what should not the custom of fifteen hundred years, and the decrees of many hundred Pastors, gain of reasonable, modest, and humble men?
> (Rheims New Testament 1582, annotation on 1 Cor 11:16)

These words at once strike the core of the heart and, as if addressed to our generation, illuminate the true nature of custom. It is not the customs of men which we are bound to (those which are contrary to God, that is), but the custom of the Church, a custom which does not change, because God, the source and unifier of her customs, does not change. It did not change in thirty some years, it did not change in fifteen hundred years, and, indeed, it has not changed in two millennia. We, not the custom, bear the reproach for disparaging this practice and neglecting its vitality.

St. Augustine, whom St. Thomas cites above, gives the principle in his letter to Casulanus:

> For in those things concerning which the divine Scriptures have laid down no definite rule, the custom of the people of God, or the practices instituted by their fathers, are to be held as the law of the Church. If we choose to fall into a debate about these things, and to denounce one party merely because their custom differs from that of others, the consequence must be an endless contention, in which the utmost care is necessary lest the storm of conflict overcast with clouds the calmness of brotherly love, while strength is spent in mere controversy which cannot adduce on either side any decisive testimonies of truth.
> (Augustine, Letter 36.2)

If the custom of the people of God is to be held as law even where Scripture has laid down no definite rule, how much more where Scripture has spoken plainly, as it has here? The veil rests on Scripture, nature, reason and custom together, and so it stands on a firmer footing than anything St. Augustine had in mind in his letter. And we must be clear that when the Scripture speaks, it is Christ who speaks, the Word speaking through the voice of the Scriptures as a whole: "Diversely and many ways in times past God speaking to the fathers in the prophets, last of all in these days hath spoken to us in his Son" (Heb 1:1-2, Rheims 1582). The Word who speaks through St. Paul in this chapter is the same Word who spoke through Moses and the prophets.

## A Great Cloud of Witnesses

St. Thomas, in his Reportatio, draws out this continuity of the custom across both Testaments:

> *si quis, dico, resistat rationibus contentiose, non poterit resistere consuetudini, quia nos, scilicet apostoli et Iudaei (quia ex Iudaeis sumus), talem consuetudinem, idest quod mulieres non velentur, non habemus, neque etiam Ecclesia Dei, quia neque secundum legem Moysi et secundum Christum est ut vir velet caput et non mulier. Et sciendum ex hoc quod, sicut dicit Augustinus, mos populi Dei pro auctoritate habetur*
> (Aquinas, Reportatio on 1 Cor 11:16)
>
> "if anyone, I say, resists the reasons contentiously, he will not be able to resist the custom, because we, namely the apostles and the Jews (for we are of the Jews), have no such custom, that is, that women not be veiled, nor does the Church of God, because neither according to the law of Moses nor according to Christ is it that the man should veil his head and not the woman. And from this it is to be known that, as Augustine says, the custom of the people of God is held as authority."

"Neither according to the law of Moses nor according to Christ." Such custom we have seen in the Old Testament, and it runs from the daughters of Sion and the matrons of Israel to the women of the Apostolic churches. Nor is it only the custom of the living. We are not, as the Church Militant, the sole element of the whole Body, but we share membership with those in Purgatory and Heaven, and the body has well spoken of its custom regarding the veil, a fact that I hope is well evident this far into this argument. The veiled women of every age stand behind the custom as a great cloud of witnesses.

And St. Thomas notes that St. Paul does not stop even at custom, but adds a command:

> But this, which was stated above, namely, that women should be veiled in church, I command, in order that he might thus induce them to this observance in three ways. First, indeed, by reason; secondly, by custom; thirdly, by command, which should persuade them without the other two: 'Keep my commandments and you shall live' (Pr 4:4); 'A three-ply cord is not quickly broken' (Ec 4:12)
> (Aquinas, Commentary on 1 Corinthians 11, lect. 4, n. 622, trans. Larcher)

## Truth, Not Custom

Yet we must be careful here, for custom is not the highest authority. Tertullian puts the matter sharply:

> Having already undergone the trouble peculiar to my opinion, I will show in Latin also that it behoves our virgins to be veiled from the time that they have passed the turning-point of their age: that this observance is exacted by truth, on which no one can impose prescription, no space of times, no influence of persons, no privilege of regions. For these, for the most part, are the sources whence, from some ignorance or simplicity, custom finds its beginning; and then it is successionally confirmed into an usage, and thus is maintained in opposition to truth. But our Lord Christ has surnamed Himself Truth, not Custom. If Christ is always, and prior to all, equally truth is a thing sempiternal and ancient. Let those therefore look to themselves, to whom that is new which is intrinsically old. It is not so much novelty as truth which convicts heresies. Whatever savours of opposition to truth, this will be heresy, even (if it be an) ancient custom.
> (Tertullian, On the Veiling of Virgins 1)

"Christ has surnamed Himself Truth, not Custom." This is at once a rebuke to the modern custom of the bare head, and a caution against treating any custom as absolute. St. Thomas teaches the same in his treatment of law, that the natural and Divine laws "cannot be changed by a custom proceeding from the will of man, but only by Divine authority. Hence it is that no custom can prevail over the Divine or natural laws," and he cites St. Isidore: "Let custom yield to authority: evil customs should be eradicated by law and reason" (Aquinas, Summa Theologiae I-II q.97 a.3 ad 1).

St. Cyprian adds that custom cannot be set against truth once the truth is known:

> Hence it is in vain that some who are overcome by reason oppose to us custom, as if custom were greater than truth; or as if that were not to be sought after in spiritual matters which has been revealed as the better by the Holy Spirit. For one who errs by simplicity may be pardoned, as the blessed Apostle Paul says of himself, I who at first was a blasphemer, and a persecutor, and injurious; yet obtained mercy, because I did it ignorantly. But after inspiration and revelation made to him, he who intelligently and knowingly perseveres in that course in which he had erred, sins without pardon for his ignorance. For he resists with a certain presumption and obstinacy, when he is overcome by reason.
> (Cyprian, Epistle 72.13)

Who can hold up custom as an impenetrable shield against the justice of God? Is it not the custom of men to sin with impunity? Is it not the custom from the days of Cain to murder? The devil works hard to cloud the customs of the City of God and replace them with customs which serve to thrust the greatest portion of souls possible into perdition. Indeed, the shield of custom is made of sticks and mud, and the slightest perturbation of its integrity will cause it to fall. St. Paul bids us take up another shield: "in all things taking the shield of faith, wherewith you may extinguish all the fiery darts of the most wicked one" (Eph 6:16, Rheims 1582). The shield of faith is forged of the truth, and against it the shield of custom is no shield at all. The precepts of Truth are superior to custom and destroy it.

Yet, as was shown before, the custom according to truth is that of the Apostolic Church. This custom is not opposed to the truth, but flows from it and returns to it. This custom can be called good because it comes from the force of Tradition which is from our Lord himself instead of being the handiwork of St. Paul or Tertullian, or any Father, or any other. St. Augustine, answering the very words of St. Cyprian quoted above, accepts the principle and adds its converse:

> This is clearly true, since reason and truth are to be preferred to custom. But when truth supports custom, nothing should be more strongly maintained.
> (Augustine, On Baptism, Against the Donatists IV.5.8)

Such is the case of the veil. The truth, i.e., the reasons St. Paul has given from headship, shame, creation, the angels, and nature, and the custom of the Church stand together, and when truth supports custom, nothing should be more strongly maintained. If we had both custom and truth working in concert in our own day, we would not be laboring over these essays at all, for the right order would simply be observed. That we must labor over them shows how far the custom of our age has wandered from supporting the truth.

## Not to Be Commended

There is, of course, another custom in our day, the custom of the bare head, and some, granting all that has been said of truth and custom, will yet hold it to be a thing indifferent, and will appeal to St. Thomas in its defense. He does teach that in matters of dress we ought to respect the customs of those among whom we live:

> I answer that, It is not in the outward things themselves which man uses, that there is vice, but on the part of man who uses them immoderately. This lack of moderation occurs in two ways. First, in comparison with the customs of those among whom one lives; wherefore Augustine says (Confess. iii, 8): 'Those offenses which are contrary to the customs of men, are to be avoided according to the customs generally prevailing, so that a thing agreed upon and confirmed by custom or law of any city or nation may not be violated at the lawless pleasure of any, whether citizen or foreigner. For any part, which harmonizeth not with its whole, is offensive.'
> (Aquinas, Summa Theologiae II-II q.169 a.1)

But the whole to which the Christian woman belongs is not merely her city or her nation, but the whole Church, and the part which does not harmonize with that whole is what truly offends. And sometimes the customs of a people are so discordant with Christianity that to stand out from them is not only warranted, but beneficial to the society itself. Our Lord told his disciples that they were the light of the world, and St. Chrysostom says of them, "you shall be as conspicuous to all as a city set on the ridge of a hill," and "though there be thousands of evil-speakers, no man shall be able to cast any shade upon you" (Chrysostom, Homilies on Matthew 15.11). The Christians of every age have stood out from the pagan customs around them, and the world has been the better for it.

St. Thomas, in the next article, does allow that a contrary custom may excuse. Having quoted St. Augustine that "it is unbecoming for women though married to uncover their hair, since the Apostle commands them to cover the head," he adds: "Yet in this case some might be excused from sin, when they do this not through vanity but on account of some contrary custom: although such a custom is not to be commended" (Aquinas, Summa Theologiae II-II q.169 a.2). We treated this passage in the third essay with regard to culpability, but here we must attend to its last words, for they are the more important for the subject of this essay: "such a custom is not to be commended."

Things that ought not to be commended are so because they are bad. This is not a neutral matter, as though St. Thomas were saying, 'go with the flow, it makes no difference.' He is saying that the custom is a bad one, and that those who follow it may perhaps be excused for following it in ignorance, but the custom itself is not thereby made good. The woman may not be culpable, but we should not settle for so low a goal. St. Thomas elsewhere distinguishes what is owed by law from what is owed by the rectitude of virtue, and of the latter he names two degrees, the one "so necessary that without it moral rectitude cannot be ensured," the other "necessary in the sense that it conduces to greater rectitude, although without it rectitude may be ensured" (Aquinas, Summa Theologiae II-II q.80 a.1). The excuse he grants pertains only to the first; the veil, even where no law requires it, remains owed in the second, for the reasons St. Paul gives for it have not changed, and indeed cannot change. To aim only at being excused is a kind of pusillanimity, which St. Thomas describes as that which "makes a man fall short of what is proportionate to his power, by refusing to tend to that which is commensurate thereto" (Aquinas, Summa Theologiae II-II q.133 a.1). We are called to more than the avoidance of blame. Do we not commend a bad custom when we choose to accept it and neglect to work against it when we know the better?

That those who are reared in a bad custom, thus who have been instructed in ignorance of the truth, might have an excuse for their failure in regard to the veil is self-evident. The Scriptures are near bursting with examples of the patient mercy of our Lord towards his children, and should this be any different? But when we receive instruction from God, and, neglecting to follow it, we remain in our perverse ways as if we had not received instruction, we are counted as enemies and the guilt of sin sits on us to some degree.

To the woman who has only now learned these things, then, there is one counsel above all: pray that the Word of truth would illumine your heart in humility and in love of the veil, according to the testimony of the Scriptures and the Fathers, and, sadly, against the perverse custom of our age. St. Paul prayed the same for the Ephesians, that God would give them "the spirit of wisdom and of revelation, in the knowledge of him, the eyes of your heart illuminated, that you may know what the hope is of his vocation" (Eph 1:17-18, Rheims 1582).

## The Fruits of a Perverse Custom

A Lapide saw the contrary custom of his own day and named it plainly:

> In short, Tertullian wrote a treatise (de Vel. Virg.) on this very point, to prove that all women, married or unmarried, religious or secular, should be veiled, any custom to the contrary notwithstanding, because so the Apostle enjoins. The Corinthians he says, (cap. 4), so understood S. Paul, and up to that time kept their maidens veiled. Moreover, the reasons given by the Apostle apply to all women alike, so that any breach of the precept ought to be censured and corrected. In some places, e.g., maidens go abroad with the head wholly uncovered, to show their beauty and attract a husband, when all that they really do is to peril the chastity of themselves and others, and to expose themselves daily to the wiles of panders, and hence we see and hear of so many shipwrecks to chastity.
> (Lapide, on 1 Cor 11:10, trans. Cobb)

"Any custom to the contrary notwithstanding." If a Lapide could say this of his own age, what would he say of ours? After the head covering was loosened and removed, the other coverings were either removed, or nullified, or shrunk to an extreme. The perverseness of our culture, then, lies less in the one garment women have lost since their grandmothers' day than in the whole order of dress, and with it the whole order of the man and the woman, which has been turned on its head. It was indeed fashionable for a period to go about with head bare. In the age beyond the Enlightenment, worldly fashion instructed women, equal to men, to remove their covering, and take the place of their male counterparts in the world. Thus, the veil is seen as a symbol of backward subjection.

But this seemingly obsolete garment is now at the cutting edge of the true liberation of the Woman. Far from 'binding' her, the veil frees her from the grasping of Eve, from the fashions of a world that would make her a thing to be looked upon, and from the lie that her dignity lies in becoming like men, a liberty to which several women will testify in a coming essay. It sets her back upon the road that leads from Eden to the Eschaton, the road on which the Woman clothed with the sun has already gone before her.

Here, too, the 'especially' discussed in the previous essays holds. St. Paul's words concern women praying, and so the custom of the Church bears most directly upon prayer. But the principle he lays down, that the custom of the Church is law and that no custom can prevail against truth, is the same principle St. Thomas applies to the married woman's hair generally, and a Lapide to maidens who "go abroad." This, too, awaits its fuller treatment.

With this verse St. Paul's argument is complete. He has given his reasons from tradition, from headship, from shame, from creation, from the angels, and from nature, and he has raised around them the rampart of the Church's custom. That custom must be appealed to demonstrates the dire frailty of the argument against the veil. Throughout our verse-wise survey of the passage on the veil we have seen that a question has followed us, which we have touched upon in every essay and set aside for its own place: whether these forces bind only at prayer, or at all times. To that question we turn in the next essay, gathering the reasons of these verses according to their reach, and hearing the Fathers who read them, as St. Chrysostom does, to mean that the woman ought to be covered "not at the time of prayer only but also continually" (Chrysostom, Homilies on First Corinthians 26.4).
