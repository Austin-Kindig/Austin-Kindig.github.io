> "All of us, without exception, are really able only to squander the grace of God and are really only unnecessary servants."
> St. Maximilian Kolbe

*He that eareth, shulde eare upon hope.*

These words of St. Paul reveal the truth of our experience in this world: that all that is, is from God, and that all increase in the field is from God. Even the most mechanistically minded farmer must admit, in the depths of honesty, that nobody can ensure a successful crop. Every farmer hopes for the sun, hopes for the rain, and hopes for the more mystical forces of our natural world to play in concert to bring a harvest. Deep down, the seeds of hope must live, even if we would like to crowd them out, cover them in weed fabric, and never need to pull their thorns from our immaculate fields, so finely dressed with the latest version of factory chemicals.

When we produce our food in the modern world, we look at the soil and everything around it as a system. We have the "ecosystem," the interior system and mechanisms of the seed as it germinates, the system of amendments and fertilizers, and so on. There is something more certain, in a sense, than hope when we operate in the world under the understanding of mere systemic procedure. Understanding the system, we exact our will through the witchcraft of applying those amendments, genetically modifying those seeds, and controlling the weather to the extent needed to extract our desired product from that system. In other words, our notions of machine and factory have splooshed outside the walls of the factory and into the depths of our psyche. We now think of the human mind as the firing of synapses and the chemical processes associated with it, and where is the human spirit in this framework? It is no wonder that a more Christian age asked first whether a man's trouble was of the soul, and sent him to the priest before the physician. It was rightly understood that God and the order of the cosmos above the material world was not only a reality, but a more real reality, a more fundamental one, being that much closer to God in likeness. Today, we too often treat the soul as though it were only chemistry.

Hope appears to be under attack in our modern world. Where is there room for hope in determinism's fated orientation toward the fabric of reality? Where is hope's vigor when we are left to assume that all are fundamentally good? And when we have reduced our understanding of the world we live in to a set of mechanical processes, where is hope in the Machine? After all, if I know that condition x and ingredient y render effect z, where is there room for any hoping? We are devoid of all hope.

> From the highest spire of contentment
> My fortune is thrown;

Our imagination bears the load of hope's cargo, rendering well-founded hopes a safe port and ill-founded ones a fateful capsizing. In the realm of technique, imagination has been essentially done away with. Sure, we imagine the future or present realities to some extent, but not with the room and leeway that our ancestors had. We imagine within our imagination that we have the ability to comprehend all things, were we educated enough or given enough time, and so our imagination is castrated by such fancies; it goes about looking vaguely the same, though stripped of its virility. I think this was partly a result of Christian thought turning from Plato's gaze upward toward Aristotle's attention to the natural world, as we became more and more convinced that the material world was the world *in toto*, and partly the successes and progress of the natural sciences, proving the pudding, as it were. As such, we have essentially concluded that anything remotely ethereal is probably just random noise in the airwaves of evolutionary processes, and that human reason, so purified by academic pursuits, can attain to a godlike knowledge and circumspection of all things.

We have no room in the imagination for monsters, fairies, the fantastical-but-never-seen, hellfire under our feet, a firmament, heaven itself, or spiritual bonds in the mycelium that is human society. Reliant on a truncated form of imagination, we cannot conceive of hope as a pure vessel, as a spiritual reality.

In the realm of the theological, I would argue that Hope is harder to grasp than Faith and more fleeting than Charity, though it undergirds both virtues to some extent. Hope is the projection of Faith, its natural conclusion. Hope supports Charity, for who could remain charitable apart from the sustenance of Hope? That is not to say that Hope is the most important of the virtues, nor the most enduring, for Charity never faileth, and hope will vanish utterly when we see God face to face. Yet, hope fills us with the sublime virtue of humility, as we recognize our nothingness before God, our total dependence on His promise and paternal affection.

Hope is therefore a primal aspect of our humanity, and we must always remember that no matter what we do, no matter how hard we work and how well we apparently perform, ultimately everything is down to the will of Providence. Want a good harvest? You had better hope that your intention is aligned with the Creator-Lord of the cosmos, because that is the only way anything that is, is.

He that eareth, shulde eare upon hope: not hope upon industry, not science, not certainty, not the will of man, and certainly not the smallness of our imagination, because God's Providence is grander and higher above us than the farthest star that pricks a hole in Heaven for us to see.

That is why I chose these words for our family. In the unmoored philosophical backdrop, we find ourselves a-stage, we need all the reminders of true reality we can get, and as often as we can. We work the ground, we raise our children, and we give ourselves to the work in front of us as fully as we can. The motto asks us to do all of it without measuring ourselves by the harvest, and to remember, in the good years and the lean ones, whose the increase is.

In the age of apparent technological success and the victory of the worldview of the Machine, we all need to remember that we are not able to please God by anything that we do, and that nothing we do brings success apart from His blessing. So when we get down and sweaty in the soil with our hoes, while the sun beats down and the flies are biting, our only hope of success is in Our Lord, who promised us He cares for us.
