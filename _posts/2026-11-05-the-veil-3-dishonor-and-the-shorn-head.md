*The third in a series of essays on the veiling of women, following St. Paul's argument in 1 Corinthians 11. This essay treats verses 4 through 6.*

> Every man praying or prophesying with his head covered: dishonesteth his head. But every woman praying or prophesying with her head not covered: dishonesteth her head: for it is all one as if she were made bald. For if a woman be not covered, let her be polled. But if it be a foul thing for a woman to be polled or made bald: let her cover her head.
> (1 Cor 11:4-6, Rheims 1582)

In this passage St. Paul explicates the scope of his discourse, broadening the point between the man-woman relationship to the broadest set via 'every man...every woman.' This broad net catches implicitly women and men of all classes, young, old, poor, rich, married, unmarried, widowed, etc.

Notice also that St. Paul begins with a specific context, that of prayer and prophecy, and ends with a more broad context, that of being bald or no. This has the effect of saying 'especially in this case, do x, but in all cases do x anyway.' The use of an 'especially'-type clause intensifies the argument for certain cases without diminishing the broadest contexts.

The Rheims Fathers catch all in the veil, as we saw in the first essay:

> What gifts of God soever women have, though supernatural, as some had in the Primitive Church, yet they may not forget their womanly shamefastness, but show themselves subject and modest, and cover their heads with a veil.
> (Rheims New Testament 1582, annotation on 1 Cor 11:5)

We ought here to pause and reflect on shamefastness, which is the quality pertaining to blushing, as when one is shamed and instinctively covers their face in redness and with their hands. This disposition pertains also to the humble, knowing their place and not willing to be proud, i.e., stick out, and therefore they melt into the background, as it were. We can see the posturing of the veil here, as women cover themselves as if they had been shamed, the humility of their position preceding shame. More broadly, all of mankind does this when covering the private regions of the body, which parts if uncovered would cause one to blush and cover them as best one can.

The totality of this injunction is supported by a Lapide:

> Moreover, the Apostle here means any woman, whether unmarried, virgin, married, or unchaste. He bids all alike to go veiled. So Tertullian (de Vel. Virg. c4,5) lays down, and adds that the Corinthians understood this to be S. Paul's meaning, for up to that time, he says, they follow S. Paul's injunction, and veil their wives and daughters.
> (Lapide, on 1 Cor 11:5, trans. Cobb)

If, then, every woman and every man are caught in this net, the question before us shifts from the who to the what: what is this dishonor of which St. Paul speaks, and why does it fall so differently on the man than on the woman? Following the order of St. Paul himself, we will treat first of the man.

## Two Heads

We ought first to note that the word 'head' in these verses is taken in two senses. There is the spiritual sense, treated at length in the previous essay, according to which the head of the man is Christ, the head of the woman is the man, and the head of Christ is God. There is also the physical sense, i.e., the head proper to the body, which may be either covered or bare. St. Paul does not here separate the two senses, but rather plays the one upon the other, so that to dishonor the physical head by covering or uncovering it amiss is likewise to dishonor the spiritual head which it signifies. The dishonor so incurred climbs the ladder of headship, as it were, such that if Christ were to dishonor his head, the dishonor would pertain to the Father.

It is for this reason that the shame is felt in the body as well, for shame is manifested principally in the face, in the blush (in Latin, *erubescentia*), and in the instinct to cover what has been exposed. St. Thomas distinguishes between the one who is ashamed and the one who blushes, saying, "the man who is ashamed acts in secret, but he who blushes fears to be disgraced" (Aquinas, Summa Theologiae II-II q.144 a.2). The bodily posture of shame and the spiritual reality of dishonor are then bound up in one another, in like manner to how the veil is bound up in the order of headship as previously expounded.

Ultimately, the question pertains to the primordial and archetypal order which God has woven into the fabric of reality from eternity. The ordering of Father, Son, man, and woman is no mere arrangement of convenience, but is set into the very substrate of creation. The dishonor of which St. Paul speaks, then, inherently brings shame, as it jades or obscures that order and sets itself in opposition to the substrate of reality as it was fashioned by the Creator. To dishonor the head in this way is to speak falsely with the body, so to speak, regarding the order of things.

St. Thomas, in his treatment of the man, states:

> But it pertains to man's dignity (as will be clear below) not to wear a covering on his head; consequently, he says that every man praying or prophesying with his head covered disgraces his head, i.e., does something unbecoming a man.
> (Aquinas, Commentary on 1 Corinthians 11, lect. 2, n. 592, trans. Larcher)

Peter Lombard likewise explicates both what is signified in the bare head of the man and the kind of covering St. Paul has in mind:

> *Vir quidem, qui maior est, non debet velare caput suum, id est subiectionis habere signum, quasi non sit liber ad Deum. Velamen hic intelligendum est non omne, sed quoddam, quo utebantur antiqui, et adhuc etiam utuntur plerique alicubi, quo totum caput obvolvebatur, et est pene simile velamini mulierum.*
> (Peter Lombard, Collectanea, on 1 Cor 11:6-7, PL 191, col. 1630D)
>
> "The man indeed, who is greater, ought not to veil his head, that is, to have the sign of subjection, as though he were not free before God. By 'veil' here is to be understood not every covering, but a certain one which the ancients used, and which many still use in some places, by which the whole head was wrapped, and which is almost like the veil of women."

Recall that in the previous essay Lombard noted that the unveiled woman prays "as though there were nothing between her and God." Here we have the reverse case. The man who covers his head approaches God as if there were an intermediate between himself and God, i.e., as if he were not free, and as if he stood under some other head than Christ. This pertains principally to the veil, which is the sign proper to the woman and which the man ought not take up. Still, the principle extends also to the hat, hence the customs of uncovering the head for prayer, on the entry of a building, and at Mass. Even at secular rituals, such as the singing of the national anthem, men bare their heads, and these rituals are microcosmic echoes of the super-cosmic order, wherein the man bares his head before what is above him because his whole posture before God is one of immediate access through Christ.

One could see an instance of this dishonor in a priest at the Holy Sacrifice who fails to live up to his office *in persona Christi*. Standing in the place of the Head, if he should obscure that Head, whether by his manner or by his life, he dishonors not only himself, but also Christ whom he images to the faithful.

The man, though, at all times lives in Christ, the head of the man, as St. Paul states elsewhere: "For as many of you as are baptized in Christ, have put on Christ" (Gal 3:27, Rheims 1582). The man then 'wears' Christ, so to speak, and approaches the Father clothed in him. A figure of this can be found in Jacob, who came before his father Isaac clothed in the garments of his elder brother:

> And she did on him the garments of Esau very good, which she had at home with her: and the little skins of the kids she put about his hands, and covered the bare of his neck.
> (Gen 27:15-16, Douay 1609)

As Jacob received the blessing of his father by wearing the garments of the firstborn, so too do we receive the blessing of the Father by wearing Christ, the Firstborn of all creation, though what Jacob took by cunning Christ gives to us freely. Thus, the head of the man, though bare before God, is not entirely bare, but is clothed in that one covering which does not stand between him and the Father.

St. Thomas adds that this precept pertaining to the man concerns principally public prayer:

> The following objection is raised: For many with heads covered pray in church without any disgrace, as they wish to pray more secretly. The answer is that prayer is twofold: one is private and is offered to God in one's own person; the other is public and is offered to God in the person of the entire Church, as is clear from the prayers said in the church by priests. It is these latter prayers that the Apostle has in mind here.
> (Aquinas, Commentary on 1 Corinthians 11, lect. 2, n. 593, trans. Larcher)

It is worth noting this limit on the man's part, because St. Chrysostom, as will be shown shortly, does not set the same limit on the part of the woman, and so the two obligations are not mere mirror images of one another. The man is never commanded to cover his head, but only commanded not to cover it at prayer. Our customs of baring the head on entering a house or at the anthem extend this bareness into lesser settings as a kind of courtesy which echoes the greater, whereas the covering of the woman is bound to no particular hour at all. This asymmetry will prove to be one of the principal distinctions between the sexes when the question of perpetual veiling is given its own treatment.

## Praying or Prophesying

One might ask why St. Paul speaks of women prophesying at all. St. Chrysostom notes that the gift was indeed present among them:

> For there were, as I said, both men who prophesied and women who had this gift at that time, as the daughters of Philip, as others before them and after them: concerning whom also the prophet spoke of old: your sons shall prophesy, and your daughters shall see visions.
> (Chrysostom, Homilies on First Corinthians 26.4)

Yet St. Paul says later in this same epistle that women ought to keep silence in the churches. How, then, could a woman prophesy in the assembly? A Lapide provides a reconciliation:

> The phrase, 'Every woman that prayeth or prophesieth,' does not use 'prophesieth' in its strict, and proper meaning of uttering a prophecy or an exposition, but in the improper sense of singing hymns or psalms to the praise of God. For S. Paul is here speaking of the public assembly, in which he does not allow a woman to speak or to teach, but only to sing her part well when the whole congregation sings.
> (Lapide, on 1 Cor 11:4-5, trans. Cobb)

St. Thomas, in his Reportatio, likewise says that prophecy is sometimes taken "*pro laude divina cantanda, et hoc permittitur mulieri*," "for the singing of divine praise, and this is permitted to a woman" (Aquinas, Reportatio on 1 Cor 11:5).

Whatever the gift, the occasion is that of prayer and praise. Still, as noted at the outset, 'praying or prophesying' names the occasion which St. Paul is correcting, and especially so, rather than marking the boundary of the precept. The reasons he gives for it (the order of headship, the shorn head, nature, and the angels) do not lapse once the hymn is finished. This thread will be treated more fully in a later essay, but it is worth keeping in hand as we proceed.

## What Sort of Crime

St. Chrysostom poses the same question which a modern reader would ask:

> Thus much in answer to the heretics: but we must also orderly go over the whole passage. For perhaps some one might here have doubt also, questioning with himself, what sort of a crime it was for the woman to be uncovered, or the man covered? What sort of crime it is, learn now from hence.
> (Chrysostom, Homilies on First Corinthians 26.4)

And he answers:

> Symbols many and diverse have been given both to man and woman; to him of rule, to her of subjection: and among them this also, that she should be covered, while he has his head bare. If now these be symbols you see that both err when they disturb the proper order, and transgress the disposition of God, and their own proper limits, both the man falling into the woman's inferiority, and the woman rising up against the man by her outward habiliments. For if exchange of garments be not lawful, so that neither she should be clad with a cloak, nor he with a mantle or a veil: (for the woman, says He, shall not wear that which pertains to a man, neither shall a man put on a woman's garments:) much more is it unseemly for these things to be interchanged. For the former indeed were ordained by men, even although God afterwards ratified them: but this by nature, I mean the being covered or uncovered. But when I say Nature, I mean God. For He it is Who created Nature. When therefore you overturn these boundaries, see how great injuries ensue.
> (Chrysostom, Homilies on First Corinthians 26.4)

Note the words, "But when I say Nature, I mean God." The veil, then, is not merely one garment among others, ordained by men and subject to change, but is a sign laid down by the Author of nature himself. And lest anyone consider the matter little, St. Chrysostom continues:

> And tell me not this, that the error is but small. For first, it is great even of itself: being as it is disobedience. Next, though it were small, it became great because of the greatness of the things whereof it is a sign. However, that it is a great matter, is evident from its ministering so effectually to good order among mankind, the governor and the governed being regularly kept in their several places by it.
> (Chrysostom, Homilies on First Corinthians 26.4)

If we do not protect symbols, we fail to fully honor the thing symbolized therein, and this we know by instinct. A man who burns the flag of his country, or who lets it drag on the ground, has done more than mistreat a piece of cloth. Likewise, a husband who removes his wedding ring before going out has oftentimes already begun his infidelity in his heart. The symbol and the thing symbolized are distinct, yet they cannot be cleanly separated, and contempt for the one makes inroads for contempt of the other.

The Scriptures provide us with this lesson as well, as when Baltasar the king, feasting with his nobles, called for the holy vessels which had been taken from the Temple:

> He commanded therefore now being drunk that the vessels of gold and silver, should be brought, which Nabuchodonosor his father had carried away out of the temple, that was in Jerusalem, that the king, and his Nobles might drink in them, and his wives, and concubines.
> (Dan 5:2, Douay 1610)

That same night the hand wrote upon the wall, and Daniel read out the charge against him: "the vessels of his house have been brought before thee: and thou, and thy nobles, and thy wives, and thy concubines have drunk wine in them" (Dan 5:23, Douay 1610). The vessels in themselves were merely cups, yet the king lost his kingdom over them on account of the One to whom they belonged.

This is ultimately a question of the Primacy of Christ. We believe in God made man, who took flesh and dwelt among us, and who did not disdain to work our salvation through matter, i.e., through water, oil, bread, wine, and the laying on of hands. The one who believes this cannot then hold that outward signs are nothing and that the interior disposition alone matters, for the whole sacramental economy, and all the symbolical things so implicated, stands against such a notion. The veil is not a sacrament in the sense of the seven, though, as St. Augustine will show, it may be called one in the older sense of a holy sign bearing a hidden meaning (*sacramentum*), and it belongs to the same Incarnational logic, wherein the visible sign carries and guards the invisible reality.

St. Paul's choice of words is likewise significant, as he does not say that the uncovered woman is merely out of fashion, but that she dishonors her head. Without this symbol of power on her head she runs the danger of transgressing against both God and her husband by the act of bearing herself wrongly as her husband does rightly. That there is an inversion of the right ordering of the two sexes when the woman is unveiled is attested to by St. John Chrysostom when he states,

> But if any say, Nay, how can this be a shame to the woman, if she mount up to the glory of the man? we might make this answer; She does not mount up, but rather falls from her own proper honor. Since not to abide within our own limits and the laws ordained of God, but to go beyond, is not an addition but a diminution. For as he that desires other men's goods and seizes what is not his own, has not gained anything more, but is diminished, having lost even that which he had, (which kind of thing also happened in paradise) so likewise the woman acquires not the man's dignity, but loses even the woman's decency which she had.
> (Chrysostom, Homilies on First Corinthians 26.4)

'Which kind of thing also happened in paradise.' Here we are returned to the garden, where Eve reached for what was not hers, to be as God, and lost even that which she had. The woman who casts off the veil to mount up to the place of the man repeats, in miniature, the grasping of Eden, and like Eve she finds herself diminished by it.

Dishonor is, moreover, a weighty word in the mouth of St. Paul, who speaks elsewhere of the potter and the clay:

> Or hath not the potter of clay, power, of the same mass to make one vessel unto honour, and another unto contumely?
> (Rom 9:21, Rheims 1582)

The comparison is one of allusion only, since St. Paul there speaks of the election of God rather than of the head of a woman, yet it demonstrates the company which the word keeps. The vessels unto contumely, or dishonor, are those whom God permits to remain in their sins, as St. Thomas teaches: "as predestination includes the will to confer grace and glory; so also reprobation includes the will to permit a person to fall into sin, and to impose the punishment of damnation on account of that sin" (Aquinas, Summa Theologiae I q.23 a.3). This is the abandonment of God to our own devices spoken of in the first essay. I do not mean by this that every uncovered woman is a vessel of wrath, but that dishonor, as St. Paul uses the word, lies along that same road, and it is not a road one should wish to walk, as it pertains to the departure from Eden.

The unfittingness of the uncovered head is not a matter of feeling only, as St. Thomas, treating of the adornment of women, takes up the counsel of St. Augustine to the bishop Possidius, that he not be hasty in forbidding ornaments to the married, "except that it is unbecoming for women though married to uncover their hair, since the Apostle commands them to cover the head." He then adds: "Yet in this case some might be excused from sin, when they do this not through vanity but on account of some contrary custom: although such a custom is not to be commended" (Aquinas, Summa Theologiae II-II q.169 a.2). The uncovering is therefore objectively unbecoming, and though a contrary custom may excuse the person of the guilt, it does not render the thing commendable in itself.

Surely for this to be a fault against the woman, she must desire the man's dignity and place in the act of the veil's removal, else there is rather a discord between the inward disposition and the outward sign, a false sacrament, so to speak. If a woman were to simply not wear one for another reason, this fault should not count against her in the same way, as she does not mean to transgress her husband's authority with her nakedness.

Nonetheless, to those who know these truths the transgression would come to us were we not to act on these things once we came to the knowledge of them. As our Lord instructs, "And every one to whom much was given, much shall be required of him" (Luke 12:48, Rheims 1582). Therefore, a woman ought to cover herself on learning these things to make right the relationship between herself and her husband and to become a sign of Catholicity to the world. This organization between the woman and the man is an attestation not only to her relation to him but within herself. Returning briefly to a previous essay, it is worth remembering that it was against custom that St. Paul admonished the Corinthian women for not veiling.

## Always a Reproach

As stated above, the limit St. Paul places upon the man is not the same as that placed upon the woman, which St. Chrysostom makes explicit:

> Well then: the man he compels not to be always uncovered, but only when he prays. For every man, says he, praying or prophesying, having his head covered, dishonors his head. But the woman he commands to be at all times covered. Wherefore also having said, Every woman that prays or prophesies with her head unveiled, dishonors her head, he stayed not at this point only, but also proceeded to say, for it is one and the same thing as if she were shaven. But if to be shaven is always dishonorable, it is plain too that being uncovered is always a reproach.
> (Chrysostom, Homilies on First Corinthians 26.4)

Here we have the 'especially'-type clause noted at the beginning, drawn out by the Golden-mouthed himself: to be shaven is always shameful, therefore to be uncovered is always shameful. The question of perpetual veiling will be given its own treatment in due course, but it is here present in seed, planted by St. Paul in the very verse that names prayer, the narrower occasion framed against the broader for the sake of intensification.

Nor is the covering to be a slight one, as he continues:

> Thus, in the beginning he simply requires that the head be not bare: but as he proceeds he intimates both the continuance of the rule, saying, for it is one and the same thing as if she were shaven, and the keeping of it with all care and diligence. For he said not merely covered, but **covered over**, meaning that she be carefully wrapped up on every side. And by reducing it to an absurdity, he appeals to their shame, saying by way of severe reprimand, but if she be not covered, let her also be shorn. As if he had said, If you cast away the covering appointed by the law of God, cast away likewise that appointed by nature.
> (Chrysostom, Homilies on First Corinthians 26.4)

Yet the covering alone does not suffice, as St. Chrysostom goes on:

> But I fear lest having assumed the dress, yet in their deeds some of our women should be found immodest and in other ways uncovered. For therefore also writing to Timothy Paul was not content with these things, but added others, saying, that they adorn themselves in modest apparel, with shamefacedness and sobriety; not with braided hair, or gold.
> (Chrysostom, Homilies on First Corinthians 26.5)

Here we find again the braided hair which marked the Corinthian matron, as treated in the first essay (Thompson, 1988). So, besides the other elements of this question, we see the modesty of the woman, ordered as it is to her head, as the summit of this issue in her conduct. The woman who would contravene her nature does so out of immodesty. The woman who would become a seductress does so out of immodesty. The woman who would mount up to manhood with a short hair or head unveiled does so out of immodesty.

## The Shorn Head

The reduction to absurdity in St. Paul rests upon the premise that it is shameful for a woman to be shorn, which premise St. Chrysostom presses further:

> Having taken then what was confessedly shameful, and having said, but if it be a shame for a woman to be shorn or shaven, he states in what follows his own conclusion, saying, let her be covered. And he said not, let her have long hair, but, let her be covered, ordaining both these to be one, and establishing them both ways, from what was customary and from their contraries: in that he both affirms the covering and the hair to be one, and also that she again who is shaven is the same with her whose head is bare. For it is one and the same thing, says he, as if she were shaven. But if any say, And how is it one, if this woman have the covering of nature, but the other who is shaven have not even this? we answer, that as far as her will goes, she threw that off likewise by having the head bare. And if it be not bare of tresses, that is nature's doing, not her own. So that as she who is shaven has her head bare, so this woman in like manner. For this cause He left it to nature to provide her with a covering, that even of it she might learn this lesson and veil herself.
> (Chrysostom, Homilies on First Corinthians 26.4)

Lombard, continuing from the passage cited in the previous essay, says the same more briefly:

> *deturpat caput suum. Et vere. Unum est enim, id est tantumdem est ad turpitudinem, ac si decalvetur.*
> (Peter Lombard, Collectanea, on 1 Cor 11:5, PL 191, col. 1630C)
>
> "disgraces her head. And truly. For it is one, that is, it is just as much toward disgrace, as if she were made bald."

St. Thomas ties the artificial covering to the natural:

> It has been stated that a woman not covering her head dishonors her head, for it is the same, namely, the same thing to be deprived of an artificial covering, as if she were bald, i.e., as if she were deprived of the natural covering of hair, which is predicted as punishment for certain people... (Is 3:17).
> (Aquinas, Commentary on 1 Corinthians 11, lect. 2, n. 599, trans. Larcher)

St. Thomas here cites the Vulgate, *decalvabit Dominus verticem filiarum Sion, et crinem earum nudabit*, the same verse which is treated below in the Douay.

A modern may object that it is no longer shameful for a woman to be shorn, as many women today keep their hair short without feeling any shame at all. Has the premise of St. Paul then expired?

Still, it is first worth noting that this sense has not entirely left us. This line of argumentation needs little support: if a woman, even to this day, would be ashamed to have her head shaved, which is proved of women who undergo therapy for cancer and feel the compulsion to wear a wig or some other covering over their head, then she ought, according to the Apostle, to understand that this shaved shame is hers if she will not cover herself with the veil. There remains a general Christian-wide understanding, persistent even now, that baldness is not fitting for a woman.

Now, even in these days of the conflagration of feminism and egalitarianism, we find a cultural boon in the natural inclination of the women of this age to continue to hold their hair in regard, nourishing and caring for it, and keeping it longer than men do, for the sake of their beauty and to avoid the shame still felt in this depraved and post-Christian society. If women, who are so keen to throw off the mantle of patriarchy, yet still know the natural law, avert themselves of this shame, let it be a sign to us to whom a greater law has been granted for the supernatural covering, according to the words of St. John Chrysostom which we read above, "the covering appointed by the law of God."

And even where the blush has faded, a distinction must be made between what is shameful and what is felt as shame, for a thing may be truly shameful while a person, or even a whole people, has ceased to blush at it. St. Thomas observes: "Lack of shame occurs in the best and in the worst men through different causes" (Aquinas, Summa Theologiae II-II q.144 a.4 ad 1). The best do not blush because they do nothing base, such that "shame is in the virtuous hypothetically" (Aquinas, Summa Theologiae II-II q.144 a.4), i.e., they would be ashamed should they find anything disgraceful in themselves. The worst do not blush because they no longer regard the base as base. Thus, the absence of the feeling of shame proves nothing regarding the thing itself.

Nature is, after all, immutable, and our corruption of nature is not without impunity. The shamelessness of this age can be traced to the disordered relationship between Man and Woman in our day, i.e., the confusion of hierarchy and of the ordering of Eden treated in the previous essay. When the order of headship is forgotten, the sign of headship is forgotten with it, and the blush fades accordingly.

Ultimately, the Christian cannot allow the culture of a particular time and place to define what is shameful and what is honorable as pertains to things of nature or divine law. As argued in the first essay, if our precepts were subject to the vicissitudes of the worldly and changeable, their force would not be in the Author of the New Covenant. Christianity must therefore reject both cultural absolutism and moral relativism, and St. Paul appeals to nature precisely because nature does not change with the fashions of the times, a bulwark in Holy Writ against the buffets of error such as are prevalent in our age.

## Shame in the Scriptures

The dishonor which a woman earns for herself in failing to cover herself over flows from her neglecting her position in creation and especially before God. This was so from the beginning, as stated previously, and therefore is instructed in the natural law. Because of this fact, we see the shame of the uncovered woman present throughout the Scriptures. Some of these women merit their uncovering, while others have it inflicted upon them by the wicked, but in either case the uncovering is a shame.

The passage St. Thomas cites above is from Isaias, wherein the daughters of Sion are punished in the very part which they had exalted:

> And our Lord said: for that the daughters of Sion are haughty, and have walked with stretched out neck, and went with twinklings of eyes, and clapped their hands, walked on their feet, and jetted in a set pace: Our Lord shall make bald the crown of the daughters of Sion, and our Lord shall discover their hair.
> (Isa 3:16-17, Douay 1610)

The punishment corresponds to the pride: they lifted up their heads, and God rendered those heads bald and uncovered. This is St. Chrysostom's "she does not mount up, but rather falls" in prophecy.

This shame is attested to also in the book of Numbers, wherein the woman suspected of adultery is brought before the priest:

> And when the woman shall stand in the sight of the Lord, he shall uncover her head, and shall put upon her hands the sacrifice of recordation, and the oblation of jealousy: and himself shall hold the most bitter waters, whereon he heaped curses with execration,
> (Num 5:18, Douay 1609)

It is understood that her uncovering is to be a sign of her shame. The daughter of Babylon is likewise stripped in her humiliation, as Isaias prophesies:

> Take a mill, and grind meal: make bare thy turpitude, discover the shoulder, uncover the thighs, pass the rivers.
> (Isa 47:2, Douay 1610)

Like barbarians, these depictions rape women of their dignities, removing the veil, uncovering the hidden parts, and causing these personifications to suffer hardships for the sake of shame. Thus, for a woman to remain unveiled is to subject herself to great shame, like a woman who is sold into slavery, stripped, and put to work, which recalls the sorry state of the banished Eve treated in the previous essay.

The uncovering of a woman is also used as a weapon in the hands of the wicked, even against the innocent, as in the case of Susanna:

> Moreover Susanna was exceeding delicate, and beautiful of face. But those wicked men commanded that she should be uncovered (for she was covered) that so at least they might be satisfied with her beauty.
> (Dan 13:31-32, Douay 1610)

The vile men who uncovered her face lusted after her; it was out of modesty she covered herself, to protect the heart of men from provocation in the slightest. The bride of the Canticle likewise suffers this at the hands of the watchmen, though she has done no wrong:

> The keepers that go about the city found me: they struck me, and wounded me: the keepers of the walls took away my cloak.
> (Cant 5:7, Douay 1610)

## Torn Veils

The Church herself has suffered this violation of dignity, as St. Athanasius records of the Arians and the consecrated virgins of Alexandria:

> The few women had just risen from prayer and had sat down when the youths suddenly came upon them naked with stones and clubs. Some of them the godless wretches stoned to death; they scourged with stripes the holy persons of the Virgins, tore off their veils and exposed their heads, and when they resisted the insult, the cowards kicked them with their feet. This was dreadful, exceedingly dreadful; but what ensued was worse, and more intolerable than any outrage.
> (Athanasius, History of the Arians 55)

And this not only in the church, but also in the streets:

> Many Virgins who condemned their impiety, and professed the truth, they brought out from the houses; others they insulted as they walked along the streets, and caused their heads to be uncovered by their young men.
> (Athanasius, History of the Arians 59)

St. Optatus records the same of the Donatists in Africa:

> You have laid heads once more bare, which had already received the veil. You have stripped them of the marks of their Profession, which seem to have been introduced, as a protection against abductors or suitors.
> (Optatus, Against the Donatists VI.4)

These were consecrated virgins, and their veils were the veils of their profession, but the principle remains the same. Baldness generates a natural shame, while the tearing away of the veil generates a shame more metaphysical in its orientation, as it strikes at the thing which the veil signifies. To unveil a woman by force is to tear down, in sign, the order of headship which God has set, and to present her to the world as one without a head, which is to say, without honor.

## The Hidden Sacrament

Before turning to the honor of the covered woman, we ought to say a word about shame itself. The woman of Numbers is uncovered as a sign of her shame, and this sort of shame was also worn by our Lord when he presented himself as an oblation on the Cross for our sake, taking the shame of our sins upon himself, the symbol of which was his nakedness. "Him that knew no sin, for us he made sin: that we might be made the justice of God in him" (2 Cor 5:21, Rheims 1582).

This ought to give us pause, for if shame and ignominy were evil in themselves, God could not have shared in them without losing his Godhood. Shame, then, is not to be shirked as though it were itself an evil and to be avoided at all costs, as it rests in humility and in the vulnerability of truth. Our Lord took on our shame and did not lose his glory, but rather revealed it.

This is the shamefastness spoken of by the Rheims Fathers at the outset. The woman who veils covers herself as one who blushes, freely taking up the humility of her place, and because she takes up the lowly posture of shame, she escapes its disgrace. The uncovered woman, on the other hand, refuses the blush, and so bears the shame instead. Here the dishonor and the honor meet.

Thus far we have looked at the negative signs attributed to the unveiling of the woman, but looking to the opinion of St. Augustine we see the positive glory for the woman who humbles herself and positions herself according to our Lord's desire for her when he says,

> what the apostle meant to signify is plain, and in so far figuratively and mystically, because he was speaking of covering the head of the woman, which will remain mere empty words, unless referred to some hidden sacrament.
> (Augustine, On the Trinity XII.7.11)

These words of blessed Augustine seem mysterious in themselves when taken alone. What sacred mystery is this which he refers to? St. Augustine here uses 'sacrament' in the older sense, as a holy sign bearing a hidden meaning. But turning to the words of our Lady, the Destroyer of all heresies, we find an intimation of what the veiling of the head is to the veiling of the heart and the glorification of the Woman: "Behold the handmaid of our Lord, be it done to me according to thy word" (Luke 1:38, Rheims 1582), and "My Soul doth magnify our Lord... Because he hath regarded the humility of his handmaid; for behold from henceforth all generations shall call me blessed" (Luke 1:46, 48, Rheims 1582). Such that the Woman who knew her nakedness covered it over as a prefigurement of the Woman who, in placing herself under subjugation, though she was Full of Grace, covered herself in glory. A woman who dons the veil then not only averts the dishonor of nakedness, but aligns herself to the majesty of our Lady.

Even those virgins who give themselves to Christ and forsake all sensual pleasures with the male sex veil themselves. So we see the jealous husband, our Lord, keeps his wives covered over. One might object here that nuns are shorn, and therefore subject to a disgraceful aspect, though St. Paul has just stated that it is a shame for a woman to be shorn, which objection St. Thomas takes up:

> Secondly, he leads to something unacceptable, saying: For if a woman will not veil herself, then she should cut off her hair. As if to say: If she throws aside the artificial covering, let her for the same reason cast aside the natural covering; which is unacceptable. But against this seems to be the fact that nuns are shaved. To this there are two answers: first, because from the very fact that they take a vow of virginity or widowhood with Christ as their spouse, they are promoted to the dignity of men, being freed from subjection to men and joined to Christ Himself. Secondly, because they assume a garb of penance, when they enter religion.
> (Aquinas, Commentary on 1 Corinthians 11, lect. 2, n. 600, trans. Larcher)

Yet, though shorn, they are not uncovered. St. Jerome demonstrates this saying, regarding nuns who shave on entry into their new life:

> It is usual in the monasteries of Egypt and Syria for virgins and widows who have vowed themselves to God and have renounced the world and have trodden under foot its pleasures, to ask the mothers of their communities to cut their hair; not that afterwards they go about with heads uncovered in defiance of the apostle's command, for they wear a close-fitting cap and a veil.
> (Jerome, Letter 147.5)

With these words we see that even the women who have mounted up to the spiritual dignity of man, as says St. Thomas Aquinas, by rejecting their femininity still retain observance of the Apostolic dictate. In this we can see that it is a dictate of modesty as well as of order, a thread that runs through the whole of the doctrine. Even when they have transformed their nature, or rather, have been transformed by Christ, they keep the veil that they might remain obedient to the Apostle and hold onto the modesty of their flesh.

We touched briefly before on the teaching that the woman is in the image of God insofar as she is joined to the man, which point St. Augustine will help to elucidate in the next essay. The consecrated virgin, wedded directly to Christ the Head, comes already to the dignity of the man, and in her is glimpsed the fullness of equality pertaining to the restoration of the Eschaton, when "neither the man without the woman: nor the woman without the man, in our Lord" (1 Cor 11:11, Rheims 1582). Yet she still veils, having arrived at that dignity while remaining within the framework of the not-yet-but-already, keeping the sign of the order even as she lives in anticipation of its fulfillment.

The second answer of St. Thomas pertains here as well, that the nun is shorn because she takes up "a garb of penance." She freely accepts the very shame of which St. Paul warns, as our Lord accepted his, and she is honored for it, yet she accepts it under the veil.

In this, too, they follow the Mother of God, who, though higher than all of them in grace, still took up the veil.

The uncovered head, then, is a dishonor which climbs the ladder of headship to God himself, and a shame as real as that of the shorn head, whether or not our age still blushes at it. The covered head, on the other hand, is the posture of the handmaid, whom all generations call blessed. Yet St. Paul does not leave us with the honor and dishonor alone, but next provides the reason underlying them both: that the man is the image and glory of God, and the woman the glory of the man. It is to this reason that we turn in the next essay.
