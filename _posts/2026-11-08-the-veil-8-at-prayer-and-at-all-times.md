*The eighth in a series of essays on the veiling of women, following St. Paul's argument in 1 Corinthians 11. Having walked through the passage verse by verse, this essay gathers the whole case on whether the veil pertains only to prayer or to all times.*

We closed the last essay at the end of our walk through St. Paul's passage, having followed him verse by verse from the tradition he delivered to the rampart of the Church's custom which he raised around it. Throughout that walk a question has followed us, which we touched upon in every essay and each time set aside for its own place, i.e., whether these precepts pertain only to the time of prayer, or to the whole of a woman's life. The question is not an idle one, for in many of our parishes the veil has returned to the Mass, and it is natural to ask whether the reasons which bring it there are bounded by the church doors. To answer it we must gather up the whole of what has been said in the previous essays and see where it tends.

## One Stream

We have treated St. Paul's reasons one at a time, as he gives them, and there is a danger in this, namely that the reader comes to think of them as so many separate proofs, any one of which might be granted or denied without touching the others, as if the case for the veil were a pile of bricks rather than a mortared building. Yet the reasons are of another kind altogether, for they all flow from the same source and return to the same source, and the framing which we have kept throughout these essays bears this out: the original order of Eden, the valley of that order after the Fall, and the return of the order in Christ, who is "Alpha and Omega, the first and the last, the beginning and the end" (Apoc 22:13, Rheims 1582).

Consider first the woman as she was made. Eve was not formed with a veil of cloth upon her head. Of our first parents the Scripture says plainly, "they were both naked, to wit Adam and his wife; and were not ashamed" (Gen 2:25, Douay 1609). Yet she was not uncovered in the sense that matters, for her true veil was spiritual, i.e., her orientation towards the right order of God's creation, in which she stood under her head as he stood under God. St. Thomas describes the rectitude of that first state thus: "this rectitude consisted in his reason being subject to God, the lower powers to reason, and the body to the soul" (Aquinas, Summa Theologiae I q.95 a.1). So long as this rectitude held, there was nothing to hide and nothing that needed a sign to recall it, for the order was simply inherent in being itself. When it was broken, the first thing our parents perceived was their nakedness: "And the eyes of them both were opened: and when they perceived themselves to be naked, they sowed together leaves of a fig tree, and made themselves aprons" (Gen 3:7, Douay 1609). And God himself did not leave them in their makeshift covering, but "made for Adam and his wife garments of skins, and clothed them" (Gen 3:21, Douay 1609). The garment, and with it the veil, belongs to the valley, as a provision of God for fallen man.

The veil now, then, is needed to help us. St. Thomas, as was shown in the seventh essay, distinguishes two degrees of what is owed by the rectitude of virtue, the one without which rectitude cannot be had at all, the other "necessary in the sense that it conduces to greater rectitude, although without it rectitude may be ensured" (Aquinas, Summa Theologiae II-II q.80 a.1). The veil belongs to the second. The cloth does not of itself confer the order, as though righteousness could be put on with a garment, yet it aids her in keeping what Eve once kept without it, and it signifies to her and to all who see her the order which was, which is broken, and which is being restored. Thus the veil of cloth serves the spiritual veil, and points back to Eden and forward to the Eschaton, where the Bride is clothed by God himself.

If this is so, then it becomes clear why no one of St. Paul's reasons is the smoking gun, so to speak. Each is a stream branching from the same source. The first essay showed the veil delivered as a tradition, and it is a tradition because the order it signifies was delivered first by God in Eden. The second set forth the order of headship, God the head of Christ, Christ of the man, and the man of the woman, and this headship does not lapse when the woman leaves the church, for Rebecca veiled in an open field at no prayer, on seeing her lord. In the third we found that the uncovered woman dishonors her head, and that, as St. Chrysostom reads it, to be uncovered is "always a reproach," while the man, as we also saw there, uncovers of necessity only at prayer. The fourth traced the order to creation, to the woman taken from the side of the man, and creation is not confined to the hour of worship. The fifth brought before us the angels, who are present at the Holy Sacrifice and who, as our guardians, see the whole course of our lives. The sixth heard nature itself teach, and nature's covering of hair is never taken off. And the seventh showed that the custom of the Church stands with truth, and the custom of the Church, as the Fathers will show us below, was a custom of both street and sanctuary.

At some times one of these reasons is the prime, and at other times another, so that before the eyes of strange men sexual modesty is the prime, before her husband her subjection, before God at prayer the order of creation and the honor of her head, and before the angels the holy signification which pleases them. Yet all of these fish swim in the same stream.

## Especially

How, then, do we account for the fact that St. Paul names prayer in the fifth verse and again in the thirteenth? A Lapide, summarizing the Apostle's argument, gives the answer which holds both together:

> He replies that they ought, and especially at the time of public prayer, and he supports his decision by five reasons.
> (Lapide, on 1 Cor 11, synopsis, trans. Cobb)

We have met this 'especially' throughout the series, and in the sixth essay we put it thus, that the woman ought to be covered at all times, and especially at prayer when the mind intentfully approaches God, and most especially at corporate prayer, where the whole Church so approaches God in Word and Flesh. Let us unfold, then, why prayer, and corporate prayer above all, should raise the obligation.

In prayer the intellect is most acutely directed to God. St. Thomas, following St. John Damascene, calls prayer "the raising up of the mind to God" (Aquinas, Summa Theologiae II-II q.83 a.1 ad 2). At that moment the woman stands most directly before the One in whose order she is placed, and so the sign of that order is quite fitting then, as a servant stands most carefully in his livery when he is admitted into the presence of his master, though he is no less a servant in the field. The man, for his part, uncovers at that same moment, as we saw in the third essay, for he stands there as the image and glory of God, and the two signs together set forth the whole order before the face of God.

In corporate prayer, and most of all at the Holy Sacrifice, something more is added, for there the man and the woman approach God together, as one flesh. The Scriptures from beginning to end are profuse with wedding imagery. At the very creation of the woman it is said, "Wherefore man shall leave his father and mother, and shall cleave to his wife, and they shall be two in one flesh" (Gen 2:24, Douay 1609), and St. Paul, taking up these same words, says, "This is a great Sacrament. But I speak in Christ and in the Church" (Eph 5:32, Rheims 1582). At the end of all time St. John hears the cry, "Let us be glad and rejoice, and give glory to him: because the marriage of the Lamb is come, and his wife hath prepared herself" (Apoc 19:7, Rheims 1582), and he sees "the holy city Jerusalem new descending from heaven, prepared of God, as a bride adorned for her husband" (Apoc 21:2, Rheims 1582). When the Church gathers at the altar, the marriage of Christ and his Church is made present, and the man and the woman, standing in their order, show forth in their own persons the mystery which they are celebrating. Thus the veil at the Mass is the sign of the order of creation set within the sign of the order of redemption, so that the woman stands, as it were, within the Bride.

Yet prayer itself is not confined to the hour of prayer. St. Paul bids us, "Pray without intermission" (1 Thess 5:17, Rheims 1582), and St. Augustine, writing to the widow Proba, explains how this can be:

> When we cherish uninterrupted desire along with the exercise of faith and hope and charity, we 'pray always.' But at certain stated hours and seasons we also use words in prayer to God, that by these signs of things we may admonish ourselves, and may acquaint ourselves with the measure of progress which we have made in this desire, and may more warmly excite ourselves to obtain an increase of its strength.
> (Augustine, Letter 130.9.18)

Here we have the whole pattern of the veil latent in it. There is a prayer which is always, the desire of the heart directed to God, and there are stated hours at which this desire is gathered up and expressed in signs. The stated hours do not replace the continual prayer, but serve it, and the continual prayer is what gives the stated hours their meaning. So it is with the veil, for there is an order in which the woman always stands, and there are times, above all the Holy Sacrifice of the Mass, when that order is most solemnly set forth. Thus the 'especially' does not confine the veil to those hours, but signifies that what is always true is then most fully manifested.

## At Prayer

That the veil belongs to the time of prayer has never been in question in the Church, and the witness is unbroken from the earliest centuries until our own grandmothers' days. Tertullian, writing at the turn of the third century, tells us that the only matter disputed in the churches was whether virgins were bound as well as married women:

> But that point which is promiscuously observed throughout the churches, whether virgins ought to be veiled or no, must be treated of.
> (Tertullian, On Prayer 21)

The covering of married women at prayer was simply assumed. And in the next chapter Tertullian argues in a manner which ought to strike us. He argues from the street to the church:

> Why do you denude before God what you cover before men? Will you be more modest in public than in the church?
> (Tertullian, On Prayer 22)

He addresses virgins who covered themselves in public but uncovered in the assembly, and he takes the street veil as given and reasons from it to compel them to veil in church. Our own situation is the reverse of his, for where the veil survives among us at all, it survives at the church, and it is the street which has been given up. Yet Tertullian's question turns just as well the other way, for if the woman covers before God, why should she uncover before men?

The church orders of the early centuries set down the practice as a rule of the assembly. The Apostolic Tradition, among its instructions for the prayer of the faithful, says:

> And let all the women have their heads covered with an opaque cloth, not with a veil of thin linen, for this is not a true covering.
> (Hippolytus, Apostolic Tradition 18:5, trans. Easton)

When the newly converted Bulgarians asked Pope Nicholas I whether a woman should stand in church with her head veiled or unveiled, he answered with St. Paul himself: "As to whether a woman should stand in the church with her head veiled or unveiled, the Apostle teaches: If a woman prays or prophesies without her head veiled, she brings shame upon her own head" (Nicholas I, Responses to the Bulgarians 58). Gratian carried the same teaching into the Church's book of law, drawing on Ambrosiaster, that the woman "*in ecclesia propter reuerentiam episcopalem non habeat caput liberum, sed uelamine tectum*," i.e., "in church, out of reverence for the bishop, let her not have her head free, but covered with a veil" (Gratian, Decretum C.33 q.5 c.19). His source's opening words want the qualification we gave in the fourth essay concerning the image of God, but the rule itself stood in the law of the Church from that time forward. St. Bonaventure, in the thirteenth century, could take it for granted in an argument about something else entirely, speaking of women as those "for whom it is fitting always to have the head 'veiled'" (Bonaventure, Commentary on the Sentences IV, d.25, a.2, q.1). And St. Charles Borromeo, reforming the churches of Milan after the Council of Trent, set men at the doors to see "*che le donne entrassero col capo velato*," "that the women should enter with the head veiled" (Possevino, Discorsi della vita et attioni di Carlo Borromeo).

The Code of Canon Law of 1917 gathered all of this into a single canon, which governed the Latin Church within living memory:

> *mulieres autem, capite cooperto et modeste vestitae, maxime cum ad mensam Dominicam accedunt.*
> (Codex Iuris Canonici 1917, can. 1262 §2)
>
> "women, however, shall have the head covered and be modestly dressed, especially when they approach the table of the Lord."

The canon speaks to assistance at the sacred rites, and so it neither commands nor forbids the wider practice. But notice that even here the 'especially' appears, 'maxime,' when she approaches the Lord's table. The Church's law, at its most precise, keeps the same gradation which we have found in St. Paul and the Fathers.

## Why Only at Church?

Here, then, we must put Tertullian's question in its reversed form. If the woman ought to be veiled at church, why not elsewhere? The common answer, when one is given, is that we dress specially for church, and the veil is simply part of our Sunday best, as a man wears a suit to Mass and work clothes on Monday. This answer has something true in it, for there is a festive dignity proper to the Lord's day and to the house of God, and it is right that we mark it in our dress. Yet it does not answer the question proper, for the veil is a sign of a relation rather than a garment of festivity, and relations do not change with the occasion. The suit marks the dignity of the day, whereas the veil marks the order in which the woman stands, and she stands in that order on Monday as much as on Sunday. The man's suit is not a sign that he is the head of his wife on Sundays only, and neither is the woman's veil a sign that she is under her head only at Mass. If the veil were a matter of festive dress, St. Paul would have argued from festivity, but he argued from headship, from creation, from the angels, and from nature.

There is, besides, a deeper reason, which pertains to the integrity of the person. Signs ought to be congruent with the interior reality they signify, and a sign worn in one place and laid aside in another implies that the reality itself is put on and laid aside. St. Clement of Alexandria saw this already at the end of the second century:

> Such ought those who are consecrated to Christ appear, and frame themselves in their whole life, as they fashion themselves in the church for the sake of gravity; and to be, not to seem such, so meek, so pious, so loving. But now I know not how people change their fashions and manners with the place. As they say that polypi, assimilated to the rocks to which they adhere, are in colour such as they; so, laying aside the inspiration of the assembly, after their departure from it, they become like others with whom they associate.
> (Clement of Alexandria, Paedagogus III.11)

We see the duplicity, at least outward, of those who, going to Mass, dress and hold themselves differently than they do in the world. I am not talking here about men who wear suits on Sunday and blue collars at work, but men who would not dare yell at or spank their children in front of the Church, for fear of looking evil in the Church, but would come home to be most vicious and domineering. Or one could imagine the character that seems so chivalrous in good company, but is crude and saucy with companions. Likewise many women who would wear long sleeves, high cut dresses, and ankle length skirts to Church would turn around and wear pants, shorts, and revealing clothing immediately after, as if they were two souls living in one body. The polypus changes its colour to match its rock, yet the Christian ought not to be a creature of his surroundings, but the same man in the sanctuary and in the street, since in both he is in actuality the same man before God.

Moreover, the sign teaches the interior as well as expressing it. St. Thomas explains why the worship of God makes use of bodily things at all: "the human mind, in order to be united to God, needs to be guided by the sensible world," and so we use them "that man's mind may be aroused thereby, as by signs, to the spiritual acts by means of which he is united to God" (Aquinas, Summa Theologiae II-II q.81 a.7). The woman who puts on her veil each morning is admonished each morning of the order in which she stands, and the sign, worn faithfully, forms in her the very disposition it signifies. To keep the sign only at church is to keep this formation only at church, and to leave the rest of the week to be formed by other signs, which the world is ever ready to supply.

## Abroad

That the veil was worn abroad as well as in church is not merely our conjecture, being the plain witness of the Fathers, who read St. Paul's reasons as reaching the street. St. Clement, in the same chapter quoted above, joins the two halves of our question together:

> Woman and man are to go to church decently attired, with natural step, embracing silence, possessing unfeigned love, pure in body, pure in heart, fit to pray to God. Let the woman observe this, further. Let her be entirely covered, unless she happen to be at home. For that style of dress is grave, and protects from being gazed at. And she will never fall, who puts before her eyes modesty, and her shawl; nor will she invite another to fall into sin by uncovering her face. For this is the wish of the Word, since it is becoming for her to pray veiled.
> (Clement of Alexandria, Paedagogus III.11)

She is to pray veiled, and she is to be covered abroad, and the reason given for the second is the same reason given for the first, "the wish of the Word." St. Clement excepts the home, and what the veil asks of a woman within her own house we will consider when we come to the wearing of it in our own days. Elsewhere he says that "it has also been enjoined that the head should be veiled and the face covered; for it is a wicked thing for beauty to be a snare to men" (Clement of Alexandria, Paedagogus II.11).

Tertullian, to the women who labored over their hair, says simply, "God bids you be veiled" (Tertullian, On the Apparel of Women II.7), and he admonishes married women "not to outgrow so far the discipline of the veil, not even in a moment of an hour" (Tertullian, On the Veiling of Virgins 17). In the sixth essay we heard him say that "it is incumbent, then, at all times and in every place, to walk mindful of the law," and in the chapter before that he exhorts the virgin in words which apply to every woman who goes out among men:

> Put on the panoply of modesty; surround yourself with the stockade of bashfulness; rear a rampart for your sex, which must neither allow your own eyes egress nor ingress to other people's.
> (Tertullian, On the Veiling of Virgins 16)

The church orders which gave us the veil at the assembly give it to us in the street as well. The Didascalia, in the third century, instructs the Christian wife:

> And when thou walkest in the street, cover thy head with thy robe, that by reason of thy veil thy great beauty may be hidden. And adorn not thy natural face; but walk with downcast looks, being veiled.
> (Didascalia Apostolorum 3, trans. Connolly)

The same rule passed into the Apostolic Constitutions of the fourth century: "when you are in the streets, cover your head; for by such a covering you will avoid being viewed of idle persons" (Apostolic Constitutions I.8). The Didascalia adds a further reason, that "it behoves women by a veil of modesty and humility to shew (their) fear of God, for the conversion and the increase of faith of them that are without, (both) of men and women" (Didascalia Apostolorum 3, trans. Connolly). This is a real reason, though a secondary one, for the veiled woman in the street is a witness to those outside the Church, a small part of that city set on a hill of which we spoke in the last essay. And the Didascalia draws out the inconsistency of the half-covered woman with some force: "For if thou coverest thy face from strange men with a veil of modesty, how then canst thou go in with strange men to a bath?" (Didascalia Apostolorum 3, trans. Connolly), for the modesty of the veil must be the modesty of the whole woman, the congruence of sign and the thing signified.

St. Ambrose shows us what the veil gives to the woman herself when she goes abroad:

> Let custom itself teach us. A woman covers her face with a veil for this reason, that in public her modesty may be safe. That her face may not easily meet the gaze of a youth, let her be covered with the nuptial veil, so that not even in chance meetings she might be exposed to the wounding of another or of herself, though the wound of either were indeed hers. But if she cover her head with a veil that she may not accidentally see or be seen (for when the head is veiled the face is hidden), how much more ought she to cover herself with the veil of modesty, so as even in public to have her own secret place.
> (Ambrose, Concerning Repentance I.14.69)

The veil is a kind of recollection carried into the world, a cloister for the lay-woman. It protects her from wandering eyes, both the eyes of others and her own, and it reminds her of the presence of God in the manner of the sacramentals, which by a visible thing recall the soul to an invisible one. Our Lord bids us, when we pray, to enter into our chamber and shut the door, and the veil is, as it were, a chamber which the woman may carry with her into the street.

St. Chrysostom, whom we have heard throughout this series, gives the clearest statement of all, and it is no accident that he draws it from St. Paul's own words:

> Well then: the man he compels not to be always uncovered, but only when he prays. For every man, says he, praying or prophesying, having his head covered, dishonors his head. But the woman he commands to be at all times covered. Wherefore also having said, Every woman that prays or prophesies with her head unveiled, dishonors her head, he stayed not at this point only, but also proceeded to say, for it is one and the same thing as if she were shaven. But if to be shaven is always dishonorable, it is plain too that being uncovered is always a reproach. And not even with this only was he content, but added again, saying, The woman ought to have a sign of authority on her head, because of the angels. He signifies that not at the time of prayer only but also continually, she ought to be covered.
> (Chrysostom, Homilies on First Corinthians 26.4)

The man's obligation is tied by the Apostle to prayer; the woman's is tied to the shorn head, which is always shameful, and to the angels, who are always present. And St. Chrysostom goes on to draw out what this means for the rest of her conduct:

> But I fear lest having assumed the dress, yet in their deeds some of our women should be found immodest and in other ways uncovered. For therefore also writing to Timothy Paul was not content with these things, but added others, saying, that they adorn themselves in modest apparel, with shamefacedness and sobriety; not with braided hair, or gold. For if one ought not to have the head bare, but everywhere to carry about the token of authority, much more is it becoming to exhibit the same in our deeds.
> (Chrysostom, Homilies on First Corinthians 26.6)

Thus the token of authority, carried everywhere, calls for deeds which match it everywhere, and this is the integrity of which we spoke above.

St. Augustine, writing to the bishop Possidius about the dress of the faithful, shows how far this went in his own day:

> Let me say, however, in regard to ornaments of gold and costly dress, that I would not have you come to a precipitate decision in the way of forbidding their use, except in the case of those who, neither being married nor intending to marry, are bound to consider only how they may please God. But those who belong to the world have also to consider how they may in these things please their wives if they be husbands, their husbands if they be wives; with this limitation, that it is not becoming even in married women to uncover their hair, since the apostle commands women to keep their heads covered.
> (Augustine, Letter 245.1)

In this his care is not for the liturgical consideration, but for daily dress. Of note here is how he makes concessions regarding ornaments for married women, so that they might please their husbands, but explicitly excludes the bare head from this softening. Nor does he appeal to a custom of his city, but points back to St. Paul's own arguments for their authority. St. Thomas took this very passage into the Summa, as was treated in the third and seventh essays, and he too speaks there of the married woman's hair generally, not of the time of prayer alone.

St. Isidore of Seville, in the seventh century, describes the matron's mantle drawn over the head as the sign of her husband's headship, "*signum enim maritalis dignitatis et potestatis in eo est. Caput enim mulieris vir est; inde et super caput mulieris est*," i.e., "for in it is the sign of marital dignity and power. For the head of the woman is the man; hence it is also over the head of the woman" (Isidore, Etymologies XIX.25.4). We met this passage in the second essay, and it bears repeating here, for it shows that the veil of the married woman was her ordinary dress, worn wherever she went, and that its meaning was understood to be St. Paul's. When the veil begins, and how it pertains to maidens and virgins, is a question with its own history, which we will take up in the next essay.

Nor did the practice die with the Fathers. St. Bernardine of Siena, preaching in 1427, could hold up the women of Rome, who "all go dressed in linen, for their magnificence they all wear white linen, on back and head, the wives of princes no less than other women" (Bernardine of Siena, Sermons on Wives and Widows). And at the beginning of the eighteenth century St. John Baptist de La Salle, writing a book of manners for the poor children of his schools, taught that "women must also take great care to cover their body decently and to keep their face veiled" (La Salle, The Rules of Christian Decorum and Civility I.13, trans. Arnandez). St. John's word is *décemment*, decently, and we should not hear it in our weakened modern sense, as though it meant merely 'nicely', or something such as that. It comes from the Latin *decet*, "it is fitting," the very word of St. Paul's question in the thirteenth verse, "*decet mulierem non velatam orare Deum?*", which the Rheims renders, "doth it become a woman not covered to pray unto God?", and the same word St. Augustine uses when he says it is not becoming, "*nec maritatas decet*," even for married women to uncover their hair. To call a covering decent, in this older sense, is to say that it befits the order of things, and this is congruent with St. Thomas, who, as previously expounded, calls a custom of non-veiling "not to be commended," for what does not befit the order cannot be commended. The covered head was, until very recently, simply how Christian women dressed by and large, in accordance with the principles laid out by St. Paul.

## Always a Christian

A Christian is always a Christian. St. Paul says to the same Corinthians, in the chapter just before our chosen passage, "Therefore whether you eat, or drink, or do any other thing; do all things unto the glory of God" (1 Cor 10:31, Rheims 1582). The whole of the Christian life is lived in the light of the end toward which it tends, and this eschatological outlook is what makes the question of the veil at all times a question about the whole of a woman's life, nay, the life of the whole family, and not only about her dress.

We live in the not-yet and the already. The order of Eden is not yet restored in its fullness, and so the woman needs the veil as an aid and a reminder, a garment of the valley. But the order is already being restored in Christ, and so the veil is no longer only a covering of shame, as the fig leaves were, but a sign of glory, of the woman who has taken up her place in the order of God freely and gladly. The woman who wears it at all times lives already, so to speak, as one who belongs to the Bride, and carries into every room and every street the sign of the marriage towards which all things are moving.

## The Case Thus Far

In the first essay we saw that St. Paul praised the Corinthians for keeping the traditions he had delivered, and that the veil was among them, delivered by the Apostle as a practice of the Church before ever he wrote a word about it. In the second we considered the order of headship, God the head of Christ, Christ the head of the man, and the man the head of the woman, an order of love and service, and the veil as its sign. The third treated the dishonor of the uncovered head, which is as the shorn head, and the shame which attends the breaking of the order. The fourth showed that the order comes from creation itself, the woman made from the man and for the man, the glory of the man as he is the image and glory of God. In the fifth we came to the angels, holy and fallen, before whom the woman wears the power upon her head, and the mutual dependence of the man and the woman in our Lord. The sixth turned to the teaching of nature itself, which gives the woman her hair as a natural veil which the veil of cloth completes. And the seventh raised the rampart of the Church's custom, which stands with truth, and which no contrary custom can overthrow.

And in this essay we have seen that all of these reasons flow in one stream, from the order of Eden through the valley of the Fall toward the restoration in Christ. The Church kept the veil at prayer essentially without interruption until our own day, and the Fathers read St. Paul's reasons as reaching the outside world, and the practice lived on as the ordinary dress of Christian women into the modern age. The woman is to be covered at all times, and especially at prayer, and most especially at the Holy Sacrifice, where the Bride stands before the Bridegroom. That is the answer to the question which has followed us from the first essay. How it came to be that so few women now give that answer with their lives is the matter of the next.
