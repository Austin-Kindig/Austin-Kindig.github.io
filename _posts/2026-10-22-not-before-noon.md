## Antipasto

Salt spray gushes forth into the crisp air, misting men as they coil ropes and patch punctured vellum sheets off the main mast. A queasy, languid merchant of Rome peers over at the meandering abyssal undulations of the undersea life, holding firm to his urban convictions. Seneca’s weak constitution here learns that some folks need to break their fast in the morning’s first moments.

Throughout the history of the Roman experience, breakfast-taking was reserved largely for around noon. Seneca testifies that the custom of the sea men in their otherwise premature repast was thoroughly justified by their grueling, demanding labors in the Mediterranean Sea. This man of reason demonstrates thereby the contextual application of need pertaining to the ethics of diet. In other words, if you need to eat, eat. The question of appropriate timing around first-foods in a day is addressed in Christian Rome as well, with Pope Nicholas urging the Bulgarians to keep the fast for the sake of Christian temperance, and St. Thomas later making a biological case for fasting naturally until midday. But ‘noon’ itself is a slippery word. Our noon is the old hour of None, which once fell at 3 pm, and how it wandered back to midday is a story told through the Church’s life of fast and feast. What emerges is an etymological spaghetti dressed with a spatial and temporal sauce, as is especially fitting for the English-speaking folk, who are neither proper Romans, nor proper Germans, nor even proper Celts, and who, here in America especially, represent the preeminent nothingness-turned-somethingness of complexified identities.

The medieval mind, as was exemplified earlier by Aquinas, directed its fasting to terminate following the ninth Roman hour of the Office. This hour roughly corresponds with our God-forsaken industrial clock time of 3 pm, or about mid-afternoon. So, to the Romans it would be numbered ‘VIIII’, to us ‘3’. History ought to stack neatly, like a lasagna, each age laid in order upon the last. Ours, however, was stirred into spaghetti while still in the pan, and here begins the confounding pasta mélange - strap in.

## Layers

For the first layer of our chronological lasagna, we will lay the foundation of the Hebrew phenomenology. In Genesis, the day is composed first of darkness and then of light. Thus, there is something of a pre-day baked into the notion of a day, and the day proper is the experience of the sun’s transit through the sky. This is not entirely foreign to us, but we will next add the second layer with the Romans’ abstract numerical representation. The first hour of the day for the Roman is not in the wee hours of the night, but is rather a marker of the prime *transitus*, dividing the daylight into ‘hours.’ Now, to this layer let us add the sauce of Christian prayer in the Office: Prime, Terce, Sext, None, and Vespers. These (excluding Vespers) mark the numerical division of the day: 1, 3, 6, 9. Vespers then marks the evening (indeed, the Anglo Church calls this hour ‘Evensong,’ such as in *The Three Ravens*: “She was dead herself, ere Evensong time.”). The astute would mark that the None hour sounds a lot like ‘noon,’ and this is no accident, as our word ‘noon’ indeed comes from ‘none.’

## Mise en Place

The question becomes: How did the hour corresponding to 9 in the Roman count become associated with 12 in our modern accounting, and similarly, how did a marker for what is 3 pm on our clock slide back about 3 hours? To answer this, we must first return to the *mise en place* from before: fasting.

The Aquinine biological account for fasting essentially condenses into this: that men, while sleeping, retain the energy (on account of not really using it) of their victuals, and men awake spend it. So, when a man has first awoken (presumably in the early morning), he has no need of nourishment on account of energies still banked like hot coals in his bowels. It is this reasoning that leads the medieval mind to question the notion of breaking one’s night fast in the early morning, as the question arises, “What are you replenishing?” St. Thomas goes further with this, ascribing the sixth hour to the loss of energies due to the expenditure over the previous 5 hours. He then turns the sixth hour into a litmus test of sorts for gluttony: ‘eat not before the time.’ This then frames his definition of a fast: a spiritual fast being nothing less than the observance out to the ninth hour: None. Here, we can see the ontological reasoning behind the Church’s fasting custom. If one is not normally to eat before midday, then just a few hours longer amounts to a threshold for fasting. So, we can now apply some of our chronological mappings to this: Breakfast ought not to occur before the Roman hour of Sext (around 12 pm), and fasting ought not to occur prior to None (around 3 pm).

## The Third Layer

Now, this is the realm within which the people of the medieval persuasion operated: fasting equates to no food before we are done with our Noonday prayers (3 pm) - and now we catch a glimpse of what is going on with the time-shift. As is testified in *The Myroure of Oure Ladye*, praying after the hours are past is of no avail, but praying early is an acceptable practice. Taking Pharisaic license with this, one’s stomach might enjoy it if one were to observe the None hour of prayer a bit earlier, thus breaking one’s fast a bit sooner. What happens if we pray our None hour about three hours earlier? Then None is about when Sext would be, and here is where our ‘noon’ now sits: 12 pm, the old Roman 6th hour. Thus, our third layer is complete.

## Provençal Spices

More demure folk enjoy their main meal in the evening, called ‘dinner’, while the simpler bumpkins enjoy ‘supper’ (what are you supping?). Dinner, though, comes from the Old French *disner*, from the Late Latin *disjejunare*, meaning to break one’s fast. Who breaks their fast in the evening? Essentially, no one. Dinner initially was held much earlier in the day, before there were such things as ‘breakfast’ and ‘lunch’, and indeed, our bumpkins still observe the word ‘dinner’ at its more *ancien* time: around Sext. What if our *disner* were to shift closer to night, however, until it were an evening affair? Why, then, we would require another meal to fill the void, such as our modern luncheon, which fancy folk fancy, or lunch as the plebs call it. And, what if to this we were to add an even earlier *disner*, calling it Break-fast? Why, it would need to be sometime in the early morning. So, at around 9 am we might have breakfast, 2 pm have lunch, and 7 pm have breakfast again (don’t forget the snacks!). The modern notion of fasting is not eating one of our snacks.

## Out of the Oven

We have now constructed our spaghetti cacophony of temporal order around the gulping, burping gullet’s jading forces. To recap: one is six, and 12 is 3 is 9, and breakfast is during the forbidden hours between 6 and 12 (or rather 1 and 6), and fasting is not before noon, by which I mean 3 (or 9 if you’d like). Breakfast proper is before noon, but after 12, and noon is 12 (so where does breakfast go?), but second breakfast is easy to place; it sits right around 7, which is after 9, and certainly after 3. Got it?

Bake our lasagna in a miasmatic kitchen for around 1000 years (stirring occasionally, and forget the recipe), and you have our perfectly sensible notions of clock-time and its relationship to eating. Eat your heart out, Seneca (but not before Noon).
